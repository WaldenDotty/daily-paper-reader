# A Measure-Theoretic Formulation of Behavioral Systems 

Victor M. Preciado, Senior Member, IEEE 

Abstract —In Willems’ behavioral systems theory, a dynamical system is identified with the set of all trajectories compatible with its laws of motion. In the linear time-invariant setting this trajectory set is a linear subspace, and its algebraic structure underpins the Fundamental Lemma: a single persistently exciting data trajectory generates the entire finite-horizon behavior. For nonlinear or stochastic systems, however, the admissible trajectory set is generally nonconvex, obstructing direct optimization over the behavior. In this paper, we lift the behavioral viewpoint from trajectories to probability measures on trajectories by representing a finite-horizon dynamical system with the set of all Borel probability measures supported on its admissible trajectories. This behavioral-measure set is convex and weakly closed even for nonlinear or stochastic dynamics, because convex combinations of trajectory distributions remain dynamically admissible even when convex combinations of trajectories do not. The extreme points are precisely the Dirac masses on individual admissible trajectories, so the classical deterministic theory is embedded as the extremal skeleton of the richer measure-valued object. On this foundation we establish two core deterministic results and outline a stochastic extension based on conditional kernel consistency. First, optimal control for a prescribed initial distribution becomes a linear program over occupation measures whose dual is exactly Bellman’s dynamic-programming recursion, with strong duality under compactness and continuity. Second, for controllable linear time-invariant systems under persistency of excitation, we prove a measure-level Fundamental Lemma: every probability measure on the finite-horizon behavior factors through the data Hankel matrix, reducing any optimization over trajectory distributions to an equivalent optimization over coefficient-space distributions. This is an exact data-driven reformulation requiring no model knowledge beyond a single informative trajectory; the classical Fundamental Lemma is recovered as the special case of Dirac measures. 

Index Terms —Behavioral systems, measure-theoretic systems, occupation measures, convex analysis, stochastic systems, data-driven control. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

The behavioral approach to systems theory, introduced by Willems [1], identifies a dynamical system with the set of all trajectories compatible with its laws of motion, called the 

behavior . For LTI systems the behavior is a linear subspace, which makes it possible to characterize the system directly from its trajectories without committing to a particular state-space realization. The Fundamental Lemma [2] exploits this structure to show that a single persistently exciting data trajectory generates the entire finite-horizon behavior of a controllable LTI system. This result has become a cornerstone of data-driven control, underpinning methods such as DeePC [3], [4],

data-driven simulation [5], and the informativity framework [6]– [8]; see [9] for a stochastic extension via polynomial chaos expansions. The applicability of convex optimization tools to control problems using the behavioral viewpoint, however, depends on the trajectory set being convex. For nonlinear systems, admissible trajectories generally do not form a convex set, so convex optimization tools cannot be applied directly to control problems formulated over the behavior. In this paper, we lift the behavioral viewpoint from trajec-tories to probability measures on trajectories by introducing a behavioral-measure set as the set of all Borel probability measures supported on admissible finite-horizon trajectories. This trajectory-level description is essential for the data-driven result developed later: it allows Willems’ Fundamental Lemma to be lifted from individual trajectories to probability measures on the entire finite-horizon behavior. We prove that this set is convex and weakly closed even for nonlinear dynamics. Its extreme points are precisely the Dirac masses on individual admissible trajectories, so the classical deterministic behavior is embedded as the extremal skeleton of this richer measure-valued object. Working at the level of probability measures on full trajec-tories serves two roles in the paper. First, it yields a single convex, weakly closed object whose extreme points recover the classical deterministic behavior and whose occupation marginals support the optimal-control formulation of Section III. Second, it enables a genuine lift of Willems’ Fundamental Lemma from individual trajectories to trajectory distributions in the controllable LTI setting. Section IV-C then outlines a stochastic extension based on conditional kernel consistency. We summarize the LTI result informally below; the precise statement is given in Theorem IV.5. Under persistency of excitation, every probability measure supported on the finite-horizon behavior of a controllable LTI system can be generated by choosing a probability distribution over the coefficient vector that parametrizes trajectories as linear combinations of columns of a data Hankel matrix. Conversely, every such choice produces a valid behavioral measure. This upgrades the classical Fundamental Lemma from a representation of individual trajectories as linear combinations of data columns to a representation of entire trajectory distributions through the same Hankel architecture. The classical Fundamental Lemma and the moment/Hankel correspondence are both immediate corollaries. Our approach is related to three existing lines of work. The first is the occupation-measure literature [10]–[15], which restores convexity to nonlinear optimal control problems by reformulating them as linear programs over measures. The fea-

sible sets in these formulations are defined by flow constraints that encode the system dynamics. The main difference from our approach is that these formulations describe the system through occupation measures on state-control space rather than through a single probability measure on the full trajectory space. In the continuous-time setting [10], [13], [14], this typically takes the form of a single occupation measure that aggregates distributional information over time, while in the discrete-time setting [15] the description consists of tuples of occupation measures, one per time step. The general moment-SOS framework underlying both settings is developed in [11], [12]. In either case, the representation captures marginal or time-aggregated distributional information and is sufficient for computing optimal costs, but it does not determine the full coupling of the trajectory law across time. In particular, a measure-level Fundamental Lemma, which requires the full joint law on trajectories, cannot be formulated from per-step marginals or time-aggregated occupation measures alone. The second line of work related to our own is the classical theory of relaxed controls and Young measures developed by Young [16] and Warga [17], which also operates at the per-step level: a relaxed control replaces a pointwise control value by a family of marginal probability measures on the control space, one per time instant. The behavioral-measure set proposed in our work is, by contrast, a single probability measure on the full trajectory space, coupling all time stages simultaneously. The third line of work related to our own is Willems’ own exploration of behavioral ideas for stochastic systems [18], which focuses on open static systems and the construction of appropriate coarse sigma-algebras. The present work can be viewed as extending that program to controlled dynamical systems through a measure-theoretic framework. Viewed through their decision objects, these frameworks differ in a way that matters for data-driven representation. Classical occupation-measure methods optimize over per-step or time-aggregated marginals, and relaxed/Young controls over time-indexed control distributions; neither description captures the full trajectory law, so neither can support a measure-level Fundamental Lemma. The behavioral-measure set introduced here instead uses a single probability measure on full trajectories, retaining the temporal coupling needed for the LTI factorization result. The main contributions are the following. 1) We introduce the behavioral-measure set MB on finite-horizon trajectory space and establish its basic struc-tural properties: convexity, weak closedness, and an exact extreme-point characterization showing that the extreme points are precisely the Dirac masses on individual admis-sible trajectories. We also relate this set to moment-SOS outer approximations by showing that the linear operator identities used in the Lasserre hierarchy [11], [12] are necessary consequences of behavioral feasibility but do not characterize it exactly at finite relaxation order. 2) For a fixed initial distribution, we formulate optimal control as a linear program over occupation measures derived from the behavioral-measure set and prove strong duality with Bellman’s dynamic-programming recursion [19]. A policy-extraction corollary recovers a measurable optimal feedback law from any optimal measure via complementary slackness and disintegration (i.e., decomposition into conditional kernels). 3) For controllable LTI systems under persistency of excitation, we prove a measure-level Fundamental Lemma: every probability measure supported on the finite-horizon external behavior admits an exact Hankel factorization through a coefficient-space distribution, and conversely. The classical Fundamental Lemma is recovered as the Dirac special case, and optimization over behavioral measures reduces exactly to data-driven optimization over coefficient-space distributions. Sections II– IV-B develop the deterministic framework, while Section IV-C outlines a stochastic extension based on condi-tional kernel consistency. Three numerical studies illustrate moment-SOS feasible-set structure, nonlinear control synthesis including a distributional-initial-condition variant, and data-driven Hankel validation. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Sec-tion II introduces the behavioral-measure set and its structural properties. Section III develops the occupation-measure optimal control problem, including strong duality and policy extraction. Section IV establishes compactness, existence of optimal solutions, the LTI specialization including the measure-level Fundamental Lemma, and a stochastic extension. Section V reports the numerical studies, and Section VI concludes. II. B EHAVIORAL MEASURES

Remark II.5 (Operator constraints and moment-SOS outer ap-proximations) . The weak operator identities (II.5) are necessary consequences of the graph-support condition defining MB.When the dynamics f and h are polynomial, these identities reduce to linear equalities involving the moments of μ, which can be enforced computationally. The Lasserre hierarchy [11], [12] can be used to enforce a finite collection of such moment equalities together with semidefinite positivity constraints that ensure the moment sequence is consistent with a valid proba-bility measure. Because these conditions are necessary, but not sufficient, for membership in MB, each level of the hierarchy produces an outer approximation of the true behavioral-measure set. Under compact basic-semialgebraic support assumptions and an Archimedean quadratic module condition, Putinar-type moment-SOS hierarchies yield convergent outer approximations of the feasible moment sequences [11], [12]. In the present setting, this convergence should be understood at the level of finite-dimensional moment projections unless additional assumptions are imposed to recover convergence of representing measures. Section V illustrates this mechanism on a polynomial example. The behavioral-measure set defines the system at the level of full trajectory distributions. For optimal control, however, it is useful to extract per-step information from a behavioral measure. This two-way correspondence is the bridge between the behavioral-measure set of this section and the occupation-measure optimal control framework developed in Section III.

Remark II.10 (Why the initial law is treated separately) .

Proposition II.9 characterizes the extreme points of the full behavioral-measure set MB, not those of the initial slice 

MB(ρ0). The distinction matters because the two sets have different extremal structure. If ρ0 is a Dirac mass δ¯x, then 

MB(ρ0) still contains Dirac masses on trajectories starting at ¯x, and these are its extreme points. If ρ0 is not a Dirac mass, however, no measure in MB(ρ0) can be a Dirac mass on trajectory space, since a Dirac mass on a single trajectory assigns a deterministic initial state, contradicting a non-degenerate ρ0. This is why the behavioral-measure set and the initial slice are defined separately.

III. O PTIMAL CONTROL OVER BEHAVIORAL MEASURES 

This section shows how the behavioral-measure set serves as the foundation for optimal control. Once the system is described by MB, optimal control reduces to optimizing a linear cost functional over the initial slice MB(ρ0). IV. C OMPACTNESS , LTI S PECIALIZATION , AND 

STOCHASTIC EXTENSION 

This section establishes three sets of results. The first concerns compactness and existence: we give conditions under which the behavioral-measure set is compact and the occupation-measure problem (III.2) admits an optimal solution. The second is the measure-level Fundamental Lemma for con-trollable LTI systems: under persistency of excitation, the entire behavioral-measure set can be characterized from a single data trajectory through a Hankel matrix. This strictly generalizes the classical Fundamental Lemma [2] from individual trajectories to probability measures on trajectories and yields an exact data-driven reformulation of any optimization over trajectory distributions. The third extends the framework to stochastic dynamics by replacing the deterministic support condition with a conditional kernel-consistency condition, showing that convexity and weak closedness are preserved.

Remark IV.10 (Degree-one moments versus the full measure) .

Corollary IV.9 extracts only first-order information from the measure-level Fundamental Lemma. The mean trajectory ¯w =R w dμ retains the classical Hankel column-space structure but discards all higher-order distributional information such as variances and correlations across time steps. The full measure-level result (Theorem IV.5) preserves this richer structure. For controllable LTI systems, the measure-level Fundamental Lemma has an immediate operational consequence: any opti-mization over trajectory distributions reduces to an equivalent optimization over coefficient-space distributions, requiring no model knowledge beyond a single persistently exciting data trajectory.

Remark IV.12 (Distributional DeePC as an exact lift of DeePC) .

Standard DeePC [3], [4] solves data-driven optimal control by finding a single coefficient vector g such that the trajectory 

w = HL(wd)g minimizes a cost and satisfies constraints. This is the special case of Corollary IV.11 in which ν is restricted to a Dirac mass δg . A limitation of this approach is that it operates on one trajectory at a time and cannot handle distributional uncertainty in the initial condition or optimize over ensembles of trajectories. Corollary IV.11 removes this limitation by replacing the coefficient vector g with a probability distribution 

ν on coefficient space, yielding a distributional DeePC that optimizes over trajectory distributions rather than individual trajectories. Random initial conditions are encoded through 

ν, ensemble objectives become expectations under ν, and distributional constraints transfer directly to coefficient space, all using the same Hankel matrix and the same data trajectory 

wd. The measure-level Fundamental Lemma guarantees that 12 

this generalization is exact: every probability distribution on the behavior is captured by some coefficient-space distribution, so distributional DeePC is the complete measure-theoretic extension of standard DeePC, not a relaxation.

Remark V.1 (Non-polynomial dynamics) . The behavioral-measure framework requires only the continuity and measura-bility assumptions stated in Assumption II.1. Polynomiality is used in the numerical experiments solely to obtain semidefinite outer approximations via the Lasserre hierarchy. For non-polynomial dynamics, alternative computational approaches include polynomial approximation of the dynamics followed by moment-SOS relaxation, sample-based approximation of the behavioral-measure set via empirical trajectory data, and Koopman-based linearization in lifted coordinates where the measure-level Fundamental Lemma applies directly.

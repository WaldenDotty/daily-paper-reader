Abstract 

Accurate estimation of thermospheric mass density is a prerequisite for orbit prediction and space situational awareness, where the upper atmosphere responds nonlinearly to solar and geomagnetic forcing across several orders of magnitude. Physics-based general circulation models resolve this response but are computationally expensive, while em-pirical models run cheaply but lack a time-evolving atmospheric state. This work cou-ples a data-driven reduced-order thermospheric model with a Kalman filter that assim-ilates in situ density observations. An autoregressive Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics with control (SINDy c-AR) reduced-order model derived from the Thermosphere-Ionosphere-Electrodynamics General Circulation Model (TIE-GCM) captures the dom-inant modes of variability and their dependence on solar and geomagnetic drivers at a fraction of the parent model’s cost. Density observations from CHAMP, GRACE, GRACE-FO, GOCE, and Swarm are assimilated across a range of orbital configurations and ge-omagnetic conditions, with a linear DMDc model evaluated as a reference. Assimilation reduces density estimation error relative to open-loop predictions, most visibly during geomagnetic storms and under single-satellite coverage. SINDy c-AR and DMDc perform comparably on assimilated orbits; on withheld orbits, SINDy c-AR is more accurate in the in-training scenarios while DMDc is better in the out-of-training 2024 Swarm-C case. Benchmarks against NRLMSIS 2.1 and HASDM (2000–2019, where available) show that empirical references can outperform the assimilated model far from the assimilated track, so results are framed as improvements over the open-loop forecast. 

1 Plain Language Summary 

Predicting the density of the thermosphere, the upper layer of Earth’s atmosphere, is important for tracking satellites and managing space operations. The thermosphere responds strongly and rapidly to changes in solar and geomagnetic activity, which makes accurate prediction hard. Physics-based models describe these processes in detail but are too slow for routine use, while simpler empirical models run quickly but track chang-ing space-weather conditions less faithfully. The method described here pairs a simpli-fied, physics-informed atmospheric model with satellite measurements to produce observation-calibrated density estimates. The simplified model represents the main patterns of ther-mospheric behavior and how they change in response to space weather; a data assim-ilation technique updates the model using density observations from satellites such as CHAMP, GRACE, GRACE-FO, GOCE, and Swarm. Incorporating satellite data reduces density errors relative to running the model open-loop, most clearly during disturbed conditions. The approach remains stable when observations are sparse and runs at a small fraction of the cost of full general-circulation propagation, supporting the update cadence used in operational drag prediction. 

2 Introduction 

The thermosphere–ionosphere region of Earth’s atmosphere, extending roughly from 100 to 980 km altitude, sets the drag environment for the bulk of the active satellite pop-ulation (Pr¨ olss, 2004). Variations in thermospheric mass density strongly influence the orbital evolution of objects in low Earth orbit (LEO) through atmospheric drag, directly affecting orbit prediction accuracy and space situational awareness (Vallado, 2001). Since the early years of the space age, thermospheric density has been inferred indirectly from satellite orbital decay measurements (Sterne, 1958; King-Hele, 1987; Picone et al., 2005). Density variability is driven primarily by solar extreme ultraviolet (EUV) irradiance, which causes order-of-magnitude changes near 400 km altitude between solar minimum and max-imum through thermospheric heating and expansion. Additional variability arises on an-nual, semiannual, and diurnal timescales (Paetzold & Zsch¨ orner, 1961; Jacchia, 1959), while geomagnetic activity further affects density, particularly during disturbed storm- 

> –2– manuscript submitted to AGU

time conditions (Jacchia et al., 1967; Liu & L¨ uehr, 2005). Even small errors in thermo-spheric density propagate into kilometer-scale orbital prediction errors over days, degrad-ing conjunction assessment and reentry prediction for LEO spacecraft. Empirical thermospheric density models have been developed to obtain long-term satellite drag observations and other measurements. Representative examples include the Jacchia–70 model (Jacchia, 1970), the Drag-Temperature Model (DTM) family (Barlier et al., 1978; Berger et al., 1998; Bruinsma et al., 2003), NRLMSISE-00 (Picone et al., 2002), and Jacchia–Bowman 2006 (Bowman, Tobiska, Marcos, Huang, et al., 2008). These models capture variations across solar cycles and seasonal timescales and dominate much of the operational orbit prediction chain. Analyses using multi-satellite orbital decay data and work on 27-day solar rotational modulation have further characterized thermospheric variability (Bowman, Tobiska, Marcos, & Valladares, 2008; Lean et al., 2006). Accelerometer-derived density measurements from missions such as CHAMP have resolved thermospheric structure and variability near typical LEO altitudes directly in situ (Liu & L¨ uehr, 2005; Guo et al., 2007, 2008; M¨ uller et al., 2009). Physics-based general circulation models offer a more complete representation of thermospheric dynamics, particularly under geomagnetically disturbed conditions (Sutton, 2018). Models such as the Global Ionosphere–Thermosphere Model (GITM) (Ridley et al., 2006), TIE-GCM (Qian et al., 2014), and the Whole Atmosphere Model (WAM) (T. Fuller-Rowell et al., 2010) solve the coupled continuity, momentum, and energy equations for neutral and ionized species. However, their high-dimensional state spaces often exceed 10 5 variables. Combined with uncertain external drivers and internal parameters, this makes them computationally expensive and challenging to deploy in real time. Reduced-order modeling (ROM) trades a bounded amount of physical fidelity for several orders of magnitude of compute reduction (Qian et al., 2013a; Mehta & Linares, 2017). ROMs compress high-dimensional model output into a low-dimensional latent space that captures the dominant modes of variability. These reduced representations are com-monly constructed using proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) or principal compo-nent analysis (PCA) (Golub & Reinsch, 1970; Rowley et al., 2004; Jolliffe, 2011). POD-based ROMs of thermospheric simulations and observations retain the dominant spatial structure and variability while enabling efficient model propagation (Matsuo & Forbes, 2010; Sutton et al., 2012). Finite-dimensional approximations of nonlinear dynamics can be identified directly from trajectory data using operator-theoretic methods grounded in Koopman theory (Brunton et al., 2021; Mezi´ c, 2005). Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD) computes a best-fit linear operator in the original state space and is interpretable as a finite-rank Koopman approximation restricted to a linear observable subspace (Schmid, 2010; Rowley et al., 2009). When external inputs such as solar and geomagnetic forcing are present, DMD with control (DMDc) augments this representation with an explicit input term (Proctor et al., 2016), yielding a reduced-order model of the form zk+1 = Az k + Bu k.The Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics (SINDy) framework (Brunton et al., 2016a) takes a complementary approach: rather than seeking a globally linear op-erator in the original state space, it identifies a sparse set of coefficients Ξ such that the dynamics are expressed as a linear combination of nonlinear basis functions drawn from a candidate library Θ,˙z = Θ(z, u) Ξ. (1) Therefore, the resulting model is linear in the identified coefficients Ξ, while the non-linearity enters through the choice of library functions in Θ. This distinguishes SINDy from general nonlinear system identification: the dynamics are not arbitrary nonlinear functions of the state, but rather a sparse, interpretable expansion over a user-defined function space. The SINDy with control extension (SINDy c) (Brunton et al., 2016b) aug- 

> –3– manuscript submitted to AGU

ments the library with driver-dependent and cross terms, enabling the model to capture state–driver interactions. The autoregressive extension adopted here, SINDy c-AR, fur-ther enriches the library by including lagged state and driver values, situating the model within the broader class of Koopman-inspired input–output representations in which tem-poral lags serve as lifting coordinates (Arbabi & Mezic, 2017; Pan & Duraisamy, 2020). DMDc is retained alongside SINDy c-AR as a linear reference model. Data assimilation combines model forecasts with observations to reduce state un-certainty in both physics-based and reduced-order models (Shim et al., 2014). In the ther-mosphere, assimilation efforts have focused on estimating neutral density and satellite drag using Kalman filtering and ensemble-based methods (M. V. Codrescu et al., 2004; S. M. Codrescu et al., 2018; T. J. Fuller-Rowell et al., 2004; Matsuo et al., 2012; Mat-suo & Knipp, 2013; Matsuo et al., 2013; Sutton, 2018). Additionally, recent work by Pe-datella (Pedatella et al., 2020) demonstrates the assimilation of ionospheric and ther-mospheric observations in a whole-atmosphere framework (WACCMX+DART), show-ing that simultaneous adjustment of electron density and neutral composition can im-prove short-term forecasts. However, assimilation in high-dimensional models remains computationally demanding, often requiring large ensembles and accurate representa-tions of model uncertainty. Coupling reduced-order models with data assimilation (Mehta & Linares, 2018; Mehta et al., 2018a) has produced efficient state estimation without di-rect manipulation of the external drivers. The framework developed here couples reduced-order dynamics with data assim-ilation for interpretable, compute-efficient thermospheric density estimation. A quasi-physical ROM is constructed using both DMDc and an autoregressive SINDy c formu-lation, trained on TIE-GCM simulations to capture dominant spatial variability and sen-sitivity to solar and geomagnetic forcing. An Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) is employed to assimilate in situ mass density observations from multiple LEO missions, including CHAMP, GRACE, GRACE-FO, GOCE, and Swarm missions. The framework is evaluated across a range of geomagnetic conditions and orbital configurations, including single-, dual-, and multi-satellite assimilation scenarios. Per-formance is assessed using withheld validation orbits and is compared against open-loop reduced-order model predictions, the NRLMSIS 2.1 empirical model (Emmert et al., 2020), and the High Accuracy Satellite Drag Model (HASDM) (Storz et al., 2005) (where its 2000–2019 coverage window permits). The present work builds directly on two lines of prior effort. Reduced-order data assimilation of thermospheric density was established in (Mehta & Linares, 2018; Mehta et al., 2018a), where a linear (DMDc) ROM of TIE-GCM was coupled with a Kalman filter to assimilate accelerometer-derived densities. More recently, (Sicoli & Mehta, 2026) introduced a SINDy c-based nonlinear ROM emulator of TIE-GCM, but without a for-mal data-assimilation layer. The contributions of this paper, relative to that body of work, are: (i) an EKF formulation that operates on the companion-form lifting of the SINDy c-AR emulator, so that the filter propagates cross-lag covariances implied by the autore-gressive structure; (ii) an analytical Jacobian propagation for the sparse nonlinear library that keeps the error covariance consistent with the first-order Taylor expansion of the AR process, so that the process noise is not required to absorb linearization error; (iii) a log-density observation model whose measurement Jacobian is state-independent, yield-ing a well-conditioned update across the full dynamic range of thermospheric density; (iv) a multi-satellite extension that assimilates geometrically distinct tracks simultane-ously in the shared latent space; and (v) a long-term assimilated thermospheric density dataset spanning 2000–2025, released alongside this work in support of reproducibility and broader scientific use. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 3 presents the math-ematical formulation of the reduced-order models and the data assimilation framework.

3 Mathematical Background 

Accurate thermospheric mass density estimation is a prerequisite for essential or-bital propagation applications such as conjunction assessment and space traffic manage-ment. The thermosphere occupies altitudes between approximately 100 and 600 km, where residual atmospheric drag is the dominant non-gravitational perturbation acting on LEO satellites. Density in this region varies by more than an order of magnitude in response to solar extreme-ultraviolet radiation and geomagnetic storm activity, yet the underly-ing physical state is high-dimensional: a global snapshot on a standard latitude–longitude– altitude grid contains tens of thousands of spatial degrees of freedom. Physics-based gen-eral circulation models such as TIE-GCM (Qian et al., 2013b) capture this complexity, but their compute cost rules out real-time onboard estimation and rapid uncertainty quan-tification. The framework developed below combines three components. PCA compresses the high-dimensional thermospheric state into a low-dimensional latent representation that retains the dominant modes of global density variability. The latent state zk and all as-sociated dynamics operate in this linear reduced-order space throughout. The SINDy c-AR model operates within this latent space to identify a parsimonious representation of the latent-state evolution under external solar and geomagnetic forcing. An EKF oper-ates in the same latent space to assimilate onboard satellite density measurements, cor-recting forecast errors and propagating uncertainty estimates alongside the state. The full thermospheric density field is reconstructed from the filtered latent state at any de-sired time via the inverse PCA projection. The remainder of this section is organized to provide context on the mathemati-cal components underlying the framework, building on prior work (Sicoli & Mehta, 2026). Section 3.1 defines the thermospheric state representation and describes the TIE-GCM training and testing datasets. Section 3.2 details the PCA-based dimensionality reduc-tion. Sections 3.3 and 3.4 develop the SINDy c library construction and the SINDy c-AR discrete-time dynamical model, respectively. Section 3.6 formulates the EKF for single-satellite assimilation, while Section 3.7 extends this to simultaneous multi-satellite as-similation.

3.5 Log-Density Measurement Model 

Onboard satellite accelerometers and neutral mass spectrometers provide measure-ments of thermospheric density that span several orders of magnitude. Rather than as-similating these observations in linear density units, which would produce poorly scaled filter gains, measurements are expressed in log 10 density. The latent state zk and all dy-namics remain in the linear PCA latent space; the log-density formulation applies ex-clusively to the observation function that maps zk to the predicted measurement at the satellite location. Log-density is more amenable to statistical analysis and facilitates in-terpretation of relative variations, which best characterize density behavior (Emmert & Picone, 2010).

4 Methodology 

The satellite datasets, the preprocessing applied to the raw measurements, and the procedure used to tune the EKF parameters are defined below; these choices determine the assimilation results reported in Section 5. 

4.1 Data 

4.1.1 Reference Model: TIE-GCM 

The reduced-order dynamics model is trained entirely on output from the TIE-GCM (Qian et al., 2013b). TIE-GCM output provides the PCA basis W, the mean field μ0,and the SINDy c-AR coefficient matrices A, B, and Ξ. It is not assimilated during fil-ter operation. 

4.1.2 In-Situ Satellite Density Measurements 

Measurements from the following missions are considered: CHAMP, GRACE, GRACE-FO, GOCE, and Swarm A/B/C missions (da Encarna¸ c˜ ao et al., 2018; Visser & van den IJssel, 2016; van den IJssel et al., 2020; Siemes, Christian et al., 2023; TU Delft, n.d.). Each dataset provides density estimates sampled at 60-second intervals along the satel-lite orbit track. Table 1 summarizes the orbital parameters of the satellite missions considered in this work. Because all LEO missions experience altitude decay under atmospheric drag, altitudes are reported as the range spanned over each mission’s operational period rather than a single scalar. 

4.1.3 External Forcing Drivers 

The SINDy c-AR dynamics model requires time series of the solar and geomagnetic forcing indices described in Section 3.1. The solar 10.7 cm flux F10 .7, its 41-day histor-ical average ¯F10 .7, and the planetary geomagnetic index kp are obtained from publicly available datasets ( Laboratory for Atmospheric and Space Physics Interactive Solar Ir-radiance Data Center (LISIRD) , n.d.; Yaya, Philippe et al., 2017; Dudok de Wit, Thierry et al., 2014). Both quantities are available at hourly cadence and are interpolated to the 60-second filter time step using a piecewise cubic Hermite interpolating polynomial (PCHIP), which preserves monotonicity and avoids spurious oscillations near sharp geomagnetic enhancements. The driver vector uk includes the current values of F10 .7, ¯F10 .7, and kp,along with the circular time features t1, . . . , t 4 defined in Section 3.1.

4.2 Preprocessing 

Several preprocessing steps are applied to the raw satellite measurements before assimilation. 

4.2.0.1 Negative density removal. A small number of raw accelerometer-derived density estimates are non-physical (negative), typically arising from accelerometer noise during low-drag conditions at high altitude or at solar minimum. These records are re-moved prior to any further processing. 

4.2.0.2 Log-space transformation. Following the measurement model of Section 3.5, all density values are converted to log 10 units before assimilation. An empirical back-ground density from NRLMSIS 2.1 (Emmert et al., 2020) is computed along the satel-lite trajectory and stored alongside each measurement for use in benchmark comparisons. For measurements within 2000–2019, a HASDM (Storz et al., 2005) reference density is additionally interpolated along the satellite trajectory (cubic in longitude and latitude, log-PCHIP in altitude, linear in time) and stored alongside each measurement as a higher-fidelity benchmark. 

4.2.0.3 Temporal alignment. The raw measurement timestamps are rounded to the nearest integer multiple of the 60-second filter step T2, and any measurements that do not fall on the filter time grid after rounding are discarded. 

4.3 Filter Configuration and Parameter Tuning 

4.3.1 Time Step and Resolution 

The SINDy c-AR model is identified at an hourly cadence ( T1 = 3600 s). The EKF operates at the satellite measurement cadence with T2 = 60 s. The discrete-time sys-tem matrices are transformed from T1 to T2 as 

AT2 = AT2/T 1 , (45) 

BT2 = ( AT2 − I) ( A − I)−1 B, (46) subdividing each hourly step into T1/T 2 = 60 sub-intervals. 

4.3.2 Reduced-Order Model 

The reduced-order state is represented by r = 10 PCA components. The SINDy c-AR model is configured with nAR = 5 autoregressive lags, spanning a 5-hour memory window. The SINDy library includes polynomial terms up to second order with cross terms 

ziuk. Ridge regression is used with α = 500 ,000, and all variables are standardized to unit variance prior to regression. The regularization strength was selected by a coarse grid search over α ∈ { 10 3, 10 4, 10 5, 5×10 5, 10 6} on a held-out TIE-GCM forecast win-dow, choosing the value that minimized open-loop reconstruction error while preserv-ing sparsity of the identified coefficients. 

4.3.3 Initial Conditions and Covariance Tuning 

Three covariance matrices govern the balance between model forecast and obser-vational correction: the initial state covariance P0|0, the process noise covariance Q, and the measurement noise covariance R. The first two were set through an iterative man-ual tuning procedure carried out over representative assimilation windows. At each it-eration, candidate P0|0 and Q values were scored against three criteria: (i) stability and non-divergence of the diagonal elements of Pk|k over the assimilation window, (ii) reduc-tion of assimilated MAPE relative to the open-loop forecast on a held-out window, and (iii) innovation magnitudes that remain consistent with the variances implied by R. 4.3.5 Experimental Design 

Each assimilation experiment is defined by a start and stop date, a designated as-similation satellite, and a designated validation satellite whose measurements are with-held from the filter and used only for independent verification. The open-loop SINDy c-AR forecast, NRLMSIS 2.1, and HASDM (within its 2000–2019 window) serve as base-line comparisons. Performance is quantified using MAPE as described in Section 5.

This configuration tests generalization across differences in orbital altitude, local time, and satellite characteristics during a geomagnetically extreme interval. Figure 3a shows the solar and geomagnetic driver time series. The period is char-acterized by sharp, large-amplitude increases in kp, driving rapid density enhancements (Thomson et al., 2004). Figure 2 compares the reconstructed density with in situ measurements along both the assimilated CHAMP orbit and the withheld GRACE orbit. For clarity, the re-sults are shown over a shorter time interval than the full assimilation period, enabling a zoomed-in view. Despite being calibrated only by CHAMP observations, the assim-ilated model tracks the timing and magnitude of major storm-time density enhancements with limited phase lag, correcting the open-loop overprediction of peak densities. Val-idation against GRACE shows a similar improvement: withheld-orbit MAPE of 22.08% under kp ≥ 8 conditions, compared with 98.80% for the open-loop forecast, 58.72% for NRLMSIS 2.1, and 51.78% for HASDM, confirming cross-orbit generalization during the storm interval. Along the assimilated CHAMP orbit, HASDM yields a MAPE of 36.74% compared with 49.00% for NRLMSIS 2.1.

5.2 Dual- and multi-satellite assimilation: November 2009 

This scenario targets November 2009, a moderate-activity interval near the declin-ing phase of solar cycle 23 during which CHAMP, GRACE, and GOCE all provide si-multaneous in situ density coverage. The concurrent availability of three satellites at dis-tinct altitudes and local-time regimes makes this period a natural testbed for dual- and multi-satellite assimilation and for evaluating cross-orbit generalization. CHAMP and GRACE are assimilated simultaneously while GOCE is withheld for validation (dual-satellite case). Figure 6 shows the November 2009 forcing and the as-similated latent state. Figure 5 compares the reconstructed densities with measurements along all three satellite tracks. The time window is shortened relative to the full assim-ilation period to improve plot readability. Assimilation reduces residual errors along both assimilated orbits and substantially improves estimates along the withheld GOCE or-bit, demonstrating cross-orbit and cross-altitude generalization. As shown in Table 2, dual-satellite assimilation reduces CHAMP MAPE from 59.94% to 5.12% and GRACE MAPE from 44.96% to 6.19%, while GOCE validation MAPE im-proves from 73.88% to 24.54%. Adding GOCE to the assimilation set (multi-satellite, three-satellite case) yields a GOCE MAPE of 3.47%, confirming that additional mea-surement streams continue to improve global latent-state calibration with limited redun-dancy. For comparison, a single-satellite baseline (CHAMP only, GOCE withheld) yields GOCE validation MAPE of 29.98%, illustrating the clear benefit of multi-orbit cover-age for cross-orbit generalization. 

5.3 Summary of MAPE across all scenarios 

Table 2 consolidates MAPE results for both the SINDy c-AR and DMDc reduced-order models across all assimilation scenarios. Assimilation yields large reductions rel-ative to open-loop for both models in all scenarios, confirming the effectiveness of the EKF correction. On assimilated orbits the two models are broadly competitive, with DMDc matching or modestly outperforming SINDy c-AR in several cases (e.g., Halloween CHAMP and November 2009 multi-satellite CHAMP/GRACE). On withheld validation orbits within the training period, SINDy c-AR achieves lower MAPE, suggesting that the nonlinear li-brary generalizes more favorably across orbital geometries not directly observed when the evaluation window overlaps the training regime. On the 2024 out-of-training test the ordering reverses (DMDc 43.71% vs SINDy c-AR 51.65% on withheld Swarm-C), indi-cating that this generalization advantage is scenario-dependent. A plausible mechanism is driver-regime extrapolation: the 2024 storm occurred well outside the 1996–2009 SINDy c-

AR training window, and the polynomial library terms zizj , ziuk, and ukul extrapolate less gracefully than a purely linear operator once the driver statistics fall outside their fitted support. Under such conditions the linear DMDc representation incurs bounded linear extrapolation error, whereas the nonlinear library can amplify deviations, which is consistent with the observed ordering. Adding satellites to the assimilation set does not monotonically reduce every individual orbit’s MAPE: in the November 2009 multi-satellite case, CHAMP and GRACE MAPEs are slightly higher than in the dual-satellite case, reflecting a redistribution of filter corrections as the shared latent state is calibrated by additional, geometrically distinct measurements. The benefit of multi-satellite assim-ilation is global coverage, evident in the order-of-magnitude improvement of GOCE MAPE once it enters the assimilation set (29.98% single-sat validation to 24.54% dual-sat val-idation to 3.47% multi-sat assimilated). The 2024 GRACE-FO to Swarm-C scenario re-mains the most challenging configuration, with Swarm-C validation MAPE of 51.65%, consistent with the difficulty of generalizing across altitude and local-time regimes in an out-of-training-period interval. In some single-satellite cases, NRLMSIS 2.1 and HASDM outperform the assimilated reduced-order model on withheld orbits, particularly when the validation satellite occupies an altitude or local-time regime not well-covered by the assimilated track. HASDM is included as a higher-fidelity reference for all scenarios within its 2000–2019 coverage; values are omitted for the 2024 Swarm-C scenario, which falls outside that window. 6 Long-Term Assimilated Thermospheric Density Dataset 

To support reproducibility and enable broader scientific use, a long-term assimi-lated thermospheric density dataset is released alongside this work. The dataset spans August 2000 through December 2025 and is constructed using all available in situ mass density observations from the candidate LEO satellite missions considered in this work. Satellite availability varies across the 2000–2025 span; the filter ingests whatever sub-set of missions is active at each epoch. The released dataset represents a continuous record composed of single-, dual-, and multi-satellite assimilation periods, reflecting the evolving observational coverage of mis-sions such as CHAMP, GRACE, GRACE-FO, GOCE, and Swarm. When only a single satellite is available, the filter calibrates the reduced-order state using that measurement stream alone. During periods of overlapping missions, multiple satellites are assimilated simultaneously (Figure 8), improving altitude and local-time coverage of the reconstructed density field. In periods with no available measurements, the dataset defaults to the open-loop reduced-order model propagation. The released product supports two primary uses. It provides a dynamically con-sistent, observation-calibrated estimate of thermospheric density that reduces residual bias relative to the open-loop reduced-order model during periods of elevated solar or geomagnetic activity. The multi-satellite assimilation intervals in particular improve cross-orbit and cross-altitude consistency relative to single-satellite calibration. Data are pro-vided in HDF5 format with metadata covering time stamps, satellite availability flags, assimilation configuration indicators, and references to the assimilated measurement sources. The dataset is hosted at (Narayanan, 2026) documenting the complete variable list, spa-tial resolution, temporal cadence, and recommended usage guidance for the released prod-uct. 7 Conclusion and Future Work 

7.1 Summary 

The framework developed here integrates PCA-based dimensionality reduction, sparse autoregressive dynamics identification via SINDy c-AR, and sequential state estimation via an extended Kalman filter. A key clarification of the SINDy c-AR model, informed by the original SINDy formulation (Brunton et al., 2016a), is that the dynamics are lin-ear in the identified sparse coefficients Ξ, with nonlinearity entering through the fixed library map Θ. The Joseph stabilized form is used for all covariance updates to guarantee positive semi-definiteness. The log-density observation model yields an affine measurement function whose Ja-cobian is state-independent, making the EKF update exact for the measurement step (subject to positivity of the reconstructed linear density at the satellite location, which was satisfied throughout all reported scenarios) and well-conditioned across the full dy-namic range of thermospheric density. The framework was evaluated across three assim-ilation scenarios during both geomagnetically active and quiet periods. Assimilation con-sistently reduces density estimation errors along assimilated orbit tracks relative to the open-loop SINDy c-AR forecast. Multi-satellite assimilation improves cross-orbit and cross-altitude reconstruction by calibrating the shared latent state from multiple orbital ge-ometries simultaneously, although adding GOCE to the November 2009 assimilation set modestly increased CHAMP and GRACE MAPE (from 5.12% and 6.19% in the dual-satellite case to 10.57% and 13.03% in the three-satellite case), consistent with a redis-tribution of filter corrections across geometrically distinct measurements. The nonlin-ear library formulation and the linear DMDc baseline are broadly competitive on assim-ilated orbits, and SINDy c-AR yields lower errors than DMDc on withheld validation or-bits in the in-training-period scenarios. On the out-of-training 2024 Swarm-C case, DMDc modestly outperforms SINDy c-AR (43.71% vs 51.65% MAPE), indicating that the gen-eralization advantage of the nonlinear library is scenario-dependent rather than univer-sal. A long-term assimilated density dataset spanning 2000–2025 is released alongside this work. 

7.2 Limitations 

Several limitations of the current framework should be noted. First, the reduced-order model is trained on TIE-GCM output; its accuracy is therefore bounded by the fidelity of TIE-GCM and does not directly incorporate observational climatology. Sec-ond, the choice of a fixed r = 10 PCA basis is heuristic and may underrepresent mesoscale and high-latitude density structures during extreme storm conditions. Third, the pro-cess noise covariance Q, the initial covariance P0|0, and the uniform 5% relative measurement-error assumption underlying R were set through the iterative manual tuning procedure described in Section 4.3, which is criterion-driven but not a formal sensitivity study. Per-mission measurement-noise budgets (e.g., based on published per-mission uncertainty characterizations) are likewise not reported here, and both simplifications may affect ab-solute MAPE values. Fourth, while multi-satellite assimilation consistently improves es-timates along assimilated tracks, cross-orbit generalization to withheld orbits remains partial. Localized in situ measurements can pull the shared latent state toward a glob-ally consistent correction, but that correction is inherently biased toward the altitude and local-time regions that the assimilated tracks sample; only with adequate spatial and temporal observational coverage does the assimilated state approach a truly self-consistent global solution. In some cases, the assimilated model does not outperform NRLMSIS 2.1 or HASDM on independent validation orbits, particularly when the withheld satellite occupies a substantially different altitude or local-time regime. Finally, the physical in-terpretation of the normalized latent states and density residuals shown in the diagnos-

7.3 Future Work 

7.3.1 Extension to higher-fidelity reference environments. 

Scenario-level HASDM comparisons are already reported in Section 5 as a higher-fidelity empirical reference. The next phase extends the framework to assimilate HASDM-derived density fields directly within the filter and to incorporate TLE-based energy dis-sipation rate (EDR) density estimates, aligning the inputs with the density products used in operational space situational awareness. TLE-derived densities are attractive because they are available continuously for the full cataloged LEO population across multiple decades, offering the spatial and temporal coverage needed to move from scenario-level assimi-lation toward a continuously assimilated multi-decade thermospheric density record. 

7.3.2 Adaptive covariance tuning. 

Although the analytical Jacobian Fk removes the need for an inflated process noise to compensate for linearization error, the process noise Q still represents genuine stochas-tic uncertainty that was set via the manual tuning procedure of Section 4.3 in this work. Innovation-based adaptive estimation methods such as expectation-maximization would provide principled online tuning of Q, particularly valuable during storm onset when the stochastic model error structure changes rapidly. 

7.3.3 Ensemble extensions. 

An ensemble Kalman filter (EnKF) or unscented Kalman filter (UKF) formulation in the same latent space would provide a more faithful representation of forecast uncer-tainty and would naturally handle the nonlinear relationship between the latent state and physical density in full-space reconstruction. 

7.3.4 Incorporation of additional measurement types. 

The framework is flexible and can be extended to incorporate other measurement types, such as GPS-based measurements or remote sensing observations of thermospheric composition (Michek & Mehta, 2026), provided the measurement can be related to the latent state through a known mapping.

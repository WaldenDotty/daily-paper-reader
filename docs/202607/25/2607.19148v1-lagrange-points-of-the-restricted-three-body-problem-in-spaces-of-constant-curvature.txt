# Lagrange points of the restricted three-body problem in spaces of constant curvature 

## Miguel Ayala ∗

Abstract 

We continue the study initiated by Kilin ( Reg. Chaot. Dyn. 4, (1999)) and by Mart´ ınez and Sim´ o(Celest. Mech. Dynam. Astronom. 128, (2017)) on the classification and stability of the relative equilibria of the restricted three-body problem in two-dimensional spaces of constant curvature, which generalize the classical Lagrange points of the planar problem. After formulating the problem as an autonomous Lagrangian system with two degrees of freedom, whose only parameters are the curvature 

κ and the mass ratio μ of the primaries, we establish several classification results for the case κ > 0 by combining analytical methods with computer-assisted proofs. These results provide rigorous confirmation of phenomena for which previously only numerical evidence was available. We also provide topological explanations for the qualitative differences between the behavior of relative equilibria in positive curvature and that observed in the planar and negative-curvature cases. Our analysis focuses on the regime of small 

μ and indicates that positive curvature has a stabilizing effect on the triangular equilibria L 4 and L 5,whereas negative curvature appears to have the opposite effect. 

MSC (2020): 70F07, 37N05, 37J20, 37J25, 65G30. 

Keywords: N -body problem in curved spaces, restricted three-body problem, Lagrange points, bifurcation, stability, computer-assisted proofs.

This paper continues the research program started by Kilin [23] and Mart´ ınez and Sim´ o [29] on the classification and stability of relative equilibria (RE) of the (circular) restricted 3-body problem (R3BP) in two-dimensional spaces of constant curvature. The goal is to understand the behavior of these solutions, which generalize the classical Lagrangian libration points from the planar case, as functions of the mass ratio between the primaries, μ := μ2/μ 1, and the spatial curvature, κ. As already pointed out in the abstract of [29], the situation for negative κ is very similar to the planar one. Instead, for κ > 0 new RE arise and several bifurcations occur as the parameters ( μ, κ ) are varied. Hence, this work considers mainly κ > 0. We also focus on the regime of small μ ∈ (0 , 1), in which one of the primaries is much larger than the other, since it is in this situation that the classical equilateral Lagrange points L 4, L5, become linearly stable for 

κ = 0. Our study is therefore complementary to that of Andrade, P´ erez-Chavela, Vidal [1], who study the same problem in the case of equal masses ( μ = 1). One of the driving motivations for this work was to understand mechanisms leading to stability of relative equilibria in the full three-body problem in spaces of constant curvature, especially in the positive case. A number of recent papers focus on their existence, [16, 15, 38], but there are few stability results. On the other hand, it is known that (at least in the planar case) the presence of one large mass in a RE has a stabilizing effect [33]. As was mentioned above, it is because of this reason that our study focuses on the regime of small μ.The motivation described above to study the R3BP is unrelated to Poincar´ e’s discovery of the homoclinic tangling that leads to chaotic behavior in the planar N -body problem as the number of N bodies increases from two to three, and which continues to motivate research these days (e.g. [20, 3, 4, 25]). In the case of non-zero curvature, the underlying two-body problem is already non-integrable, so the appropriate system to investigate the transition from integrability to chaos as the number of bodies increases is the restricted 2-body problem considered in [27, 9]. We refer the reader to [35, 10, 7] for the nonintegrability of the 2-body problem for non-zero curvature (see also [22] for discussion of secular dynamics). Even though the two-body problem is non-integrable for non-zero curvature, the R3BP, in its circular version, is nevertheless well defined, since the circular orbits of the planar two-body problem persist analyt-ically for non-vanishing curvature [18]. Moreover, these circular orbits appear to be nonlinearly stable on an open dense subset of the parameter space [8], providing a natural and robust framework for formulating the restricted three-body problem. Although our interest in the problem is mainly mathematical, our results may be useful to understand the influence of spatial curvature on the stability of the Lagrange libration points when relativistic effects are not completely negligible, as for the Sun-Mercury R3BP. For instance, our results in Sec. 8 prove that positive curvature has a stabilizing effect for L 4 and L 5, whereas numerical evidence indicates that the opposite is true for negative curvature. 

## 1.1 Contributions 

A first contribution of this work is to formulate the R3BP in spaces of constant curvature as an au-tonomous two-degree of freedom Lagrangian system in which the mass ratio μ and the curvature κ appear as the only parameters. In our approach, which borrows ideas from [31, 18], the Riemannian distance between the primaries is normalized to 1 regardless of the curvature κ. This is essential to appropriately distinguish the role played by the curvature, which constitutes a considerable improvement with respect to the normal-ization approaches followed in [29]. Our approach also exploits the full classification of circular orbits in spaces of nonzero constant curvature, which correspond to certain relative equilibria, obtained in [8]. This classification was not available when [23, 29] were written. Our set-up allows us to translate the problem of existence of RE into the problem of finding critical points of a potential function Vκ,μ (defined in 3.9) that depends on the parameters κ and μ, and is also useful to study stability. A second contribution is to give a topological explanation for the appearance of new RE for positive curvature. As observed in [29], the classical Lagrange points L 1, . . . , L5, of the planar case are non-degenerate and they therefore must persist as RE, at least for small non-zero curvature. On the other hand, the numerical results of [23, 29], suggest existence of new RE for small positive κ. The mechanism responsible for this phenomenon is clarified in the light of Theorem 4.1 (see Corollary 4.2) and may be illustrated with an even 3simpler example: consider the Kepler (or, more generally, the central force problem) on the sphere. It is clear that, in addition to the singularity at the force field’s center, there must be a singularity or an equilibrium at its antipodal point. Moreover, the distance from such an antipodal point to the field’s center tends to infinity as the radius of the sphere grows, explaining how this additional singularity or equilibrium disappears when κ = 0. In a similar manner, the compactness of the sphere restricts the number and type of RE for the R3BP for κ > 0 in a way that is quantifiable by the Poincar´ e-Hopf Index Theorem. This topological restriction implies that ‘new’ RE must necessarily exist for small κ > 0. The distance from these new RE to the primaries tends to infinity as κ → 0+ and only the Lagrange points L 1, . . . , L5 remain for κ = 0. These observations represent a significant contribution with respect to [29], where the analysis for κ > 0is restricted to the hemisphere containing the primaries and no global aspects are taken into consideration. Moreover, Theorem 4.1 is also useful in the analysis of the bifurcations of RE for κ > 0, since it leads to a restriction on the types of bifurcations that may occur. A third contribution of this work is the derivation of necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence and stability of RE that are valid for all parameter values ( κ, μ ). Our work relies on these conditions to obtain results in certain regions of the parameter space, but the criteria are equally applicable to the investigation of regions not considered in this paper. A fourth contribution is to provide rigorous classification and stability results for the RE of the problem in certain regions of the parameter space ( κ, μ ) with κ > 0. Such regions often require μ to be small. Our approach to investigating existence is analogous to the one usually employed in the planar case (see, e.g., [30]): we divide the analysis into collinear and triangular RE and make appropriate coordinate choices. Our analytical proofs are complemented by computer-assisted proofs (CAPs), which rely on the Intermediate Value Theorem from elementary calculus combined with interval arithmetic. These rigorous results are all original since, for κ > 0, and with the exception of the limit cases μ = 0 and μ = 1, the investigation in [23, 29] (including classification and stability results) is only numerical. Our combination of analytical and computer-assisted proofs is particularly powerful for the following reason. On the one hand, CAPs yields rigorous results on large regions of the parameter space. On the other hand, analytical proofs are necessary to formulate rigorous continuation arguments as the curvature κ approaches 0, since CAPs become inconclusive for small values of κ.Our rigorous existence and stability results are complemented by numerical investigations in wider regions of the parameter space ( κ, μ ), as well as by bifurcation diagrams. Some of these reproduce earlier results from [23, 29], whereas others, such as Fig. 9, are presented in terms of the Riemannian distance from the Lagrange points to the center of mass of the primaries. This approach clarifies their behavior as the curvature κ → 0. We also perform asymptotic expansions for the locations of the RE as κ → 0, showing that some of them converge to the classical Lagrange points L 1, . . . , L5, while others tend to infinity.

## 1.3 Organization of the paper 

We begin by recalling a number of preliminaries in Sec. 2. In particular, we review a geometric con-struction of surfaces of constant curvature, originally due to Montaldi, and Tokieda [31], which allows the curvature to vary continuously from negative, through zero, to positive values whlie keeping the distance between two points (in our case, the primaries) fixed. We also recall the results of [8, 18] on circular solutions of the two-body problem in spaces of constant curvature, which model the motion of the primaries, and adapt it to our setting. In Sec. 3 we formulate the R3BP in surfaces of constant curvature as an autonomous 2-degree of freedom mechanical Lagrangian system. The key point of our construction is that the curvature κ and the mass ratio μ are the only parameters appearing in the Lagrangian. In particular, we give explicit expressions for the (augmented) potential, Vκ,μ , whose critical points are in one-to-one correspondence with the RE of the system. In Sec. 4 we formulate Theorem 4.1 about the topological restrictions on the number and type of RE for 

κ > 0. We also present necessary and sufficient conditions for existence of RE and convenient stability criteria that are used in the following sections. The discussion is naturally divided into collinear and triangular RE, treating separately the cases of positive and negative κ.Our existence results for collinear and triangular RE for positive κ are respectively given in Secs. 5 and 6. These include Theorems 5.2 and 6.2, which are proved analytically, complemented with existence CAPs. Summaries of the bifurcation behavior of RE in the parameter region PμT , based on our rigorous and numerical results, are presented in Conjectures 5.6 and 6.4. Sections 7 and 8 are devoted to the stability analysis of the collinear and triangular RE, respectively. In both cases, for κ > 0, we present CAPs of stability, complemented by numerical investigations. For the collinear RE, we also provide the analytically proved Theorem 7.2. Based on our results, the stability behavior in the parameter region PμT , is summarized in Conjectures 7.4 and 8.1. Additionally, in Sec. 8.3, we present numerical evidence that positive curvature promotes the gyroscopic stabilization of L 4 and L 5,whereas negative curvature has the opposite effect. In Sec. 9, we present asymptotic expansions that determine the locations of both collinear and triangular RE in the limit as κ → 0. We discuss several directions of future research in Sec. 10 and conclude the paper with three appendices. The first appendix reviews standard results on the stability analysis of equilibria in Lagrangian mechanical systems with two degrees of freedom. The second appendix contains analytical proofs of the main existence and stability theorems and derives the asymptotic expansions presented in Sec. 9. We have chosen to defer these proofs to an appendix in order to improve the readability of the main text and to highlight the principal contributions of the paper. Finally, the third appendix summarizes the methodology underlying the CAPs developed in this work. The support code for all CAPs is available in [2]. 

# 2 Preliminaries 

In this section, we review the aspects of the classical planar restricted three-body problem relevant to our analysis, the family of surfaces of constant curvature introduced in [31], and the circular solutions (relative equilibria) of the two-body problem in spaces of constant curvature, which determine the motion of the primaries in the curved restricted three-body problem. 

## 2.1 Lagrange libration points 

The classical planar restricted 3-body problem (R3BP) is a limit case of the planar 3-body problem in which one of the bodies (called satellite ) has a negligible mass, and the other two bodies, of masses μ1 and 

μ2 (called primaries ), move on a Keplerian orbit. In this work we will only consider the case in which the 5Keplerian orbit is circular, so the terminology R3BP will always mean circular , planar, restricted 3-body problem. The R3BP concerns the determination of the motion of the satellite and is conveniently studied on a rotating coordinate system in which the primaries are at rest (and whose origin is their common center of rotation which coincides with their center of mass). The resulting mechanical system for the motion of the satellite is autonomous and has two degrees of freedom. After an appropriate scaling of units, one may assume that the distance between the primaries is 1, and the only external parameter left in the problem is the mass ratio μ = μ2/μ 1 ∈ (0 , 1]. The equilibrium points of the equations of motion for the satellite are the famous Lagrange points (or Lagrange libration points) and are denoted by L 1 . . . , L5. The points L 1, L 2, and L 3 are called collinear 

since they lie on the line between the primaries and their precise position is a function of μ. The Lagrange points L 4 and L 5 are called triangular and lie at the vertices of the two equilateral triangles whose common base is the line segment between the primaries. We refer the reader to [36] for details and historical context.

In particular, along these solutions, the distance between the particles remains constant. The classification and stability results in the case μ1̸ = μ2 are summarized below. 

• If κ < 0, then for any q > 0 there are exactly two types of RE, depending on whether the one-parameter subgroup of the isometries is compact ( elliptic RE ) or not ( hyperbolic RE ). In the elliptic RE case, the primaries execute a periodic motion, rotating uniformly about a fixed point on the geodesic connecting the primaries (center of rotation). 

• If κ > 0, then for any 0 < q < π/ √κ, q̸ = π/ (2 √κ), there is exactly one relative equilibria. If 0 < q < π/ (2 √κ), it is called acute RE . Otherwise, it is called obtuse RE . In both cases, the primaries undergo a periodic motion, rotating about a center of rotation that again lies on the geodesic between them. 

• If κ = 0, then for any q > 0 there is exactly one RE, which corresponds with the usual Keplerian RE: the primaries rotate uniformly about their center of mass.

# 4 Classification and stability of RE: general considerations 

In view of Sec. 3, the study of the curved R3BP is reduced to the analysis of a mechanical system on the configuration manifold M\ ∆± defined by the autonomous Lagrangian Lκ,μ given in Eq. (3.8). In particular, the analysis of the RE is reduced to the classification of the critical points of the smooth function Vκ,μ in Eq. (3.9) (see Appendix A). The complication is the dependence on the two parameters κ, μ .Moreover, using standard results recalled in Appendix A, the stability of the equilibrium points can be established by determining the type of critical point of Vκ,μ and, in the case of local maxima, by exploiting the structure of the linearization.

## 4.3 Conditions for stability of relative equilibria 

We now consider the stability of RE. So far, we have established that r0 ∈ M ± \ ∆ is a RE of the curved R3BP if and only if it is a critical point of the potential Vκ,μ defined in Eq. (3.9). This means that 

u0 := ( r0, 0) ∈ T (M± \∆) is an equilibrium of the Euler–Lagrange equations associated with the Lagrangian

+ ⟨e3 × r, r′⟩± − V κ,μ (r).

This is a (2 degrees of freedom) Lagrangian of mechanical type, containing a gyroscopic (linear) term in the velocities. In Appendix A we review the stability properties of the equilibrium points of this type of Lagrangians. 19 All the stability criteria needed for our analysis are given in Theorem A.3, which is formulated in terms of the following standard terminology, based on the type of eigenvalues of the linearization matrix: we say that an equilibrium is elliptic if all eigenvalues are purely imaginary, center–saddle if it has one nonzero-real pair and one imaginary pair of eigenvalues, complex–saddle if its eigenvalues form a quadruplet ±α + ±iβ 

with α, β ̸ = 0, and saddle–saddle when all eigenvalues are non-zero real. In particular, Theorem A.3 states that u0 is Lyapunov stable if r0 is a minimum of Vκ,μ , and is a center– saddle if and only if r0 is a saddle-point of Vκ,μ . Determining the stability properties of local maxima of 

Vκ,μ is more delicate since they may be stabilized by the presence of the gyroscopic term. In our discussion, we will often make a slight abuse of notation by referring to r0 as an equilibrium point instead of the corresponding equilibrium state u0 = ( r0, 0).

## 5.5 Existence of collinear RE as a function of κ > 0 for fixed μ ∈ (0 , μ T).

Below we summarize the rigorous results and numerical observations given in the previous sections, in a single statement describing the existence classification of collinear relative equilibria as a function of κ > 0for fixed μ ∈ (0 , μ T). We formulate it as conjecture, since we do not have a rigorous proof that accounts for all the details (see Remark 5.7). It may be useful to refer to Fig. 6 to recall the position of the RE with respect to the primaries, their antipodal points, and to each other. 

Conjecture 5.6 (Existence classification of collinear RE as a function of κ > 0 for 0 < μ < μ T.) . Let 

μ ∈ (0 , μ T). Then, there exist three values κi = κi(μ) ∈ (0 , π 2/4) , with κ1 < κ 2 < κ 3, such that the R3BP with mass ratio μ admits exactly the following number of collinear relative equilibria depending on the value of κ > 0.

For 0 < κ < κ 1: six collinear RE, L1, L2, L3, E2, E3, A1;

For κ1 < κ < κ 2 four collinear RE, L1, L3, E3, A1;

For κ2 < κ < κ 3: two collinear RE, L1, A1;

# Appendix C Computer-assisted Proofs (CAPs) 

The CAPs developed in this paper rely on rigorous bounds of real-valued functions over closed intervals. By rigorous , we mean that the computed bounds are mathematically guaranteed to enclose the true values of the function on the domain under consideration. These bounds are obtained using interval arithmetic [32, 37], which provides rigorous implementations of the basic arithmetic operations and elementary functions on closed intervals, while accounting for computer round-off errors. The code for our CAPs [2] is written in the Julia programming language [6] using the IntervalArithmetic.jl library [34]. For any function F : [ a, b ] → R, built from the operations and functions supported by the library, the corresponding interval implementation, FInt , satisfies 

F (X) := {F (x) : x ∈ X} ⊂ FInt (X),

for every closed subinterval X ⊂ [a, b ]. In particular, the computed interval FInt (X) is guaranteed to contain the true range of F on X.61 C.1 Existence CAPs 

The existence CAPs in Secs. 5.3 and 6.2 rely on the rigorous determination of the number of roots of the functions f1, . . . , f 4, and gκ,μ in their respective domains. As we explain below, this procedure can be reduced to the rigorous evaluation of real-valued functions on closed intervals. Consider a continuous function F : [ a, b ] → R which is differentiable on ( a, b ). Suppose that there is numerical evidence that F has a unique root in [ a, b ]. To rigorously show that F has indeed a unique root in [ a, b ], we proceed as follows. First, we verify that the intervals FInt (a) and FInt (b) have opposite signs, which implies that there is at least one root in [ a, b ] by the intermediate value theorem. Second, we find a subinterval [ c, d ] ⊂ [a, b ] on which we can show that F is monotone by checking that the derivative F ′ is strictly positive or strictly negative on [ c, d ]. Finally, we verify that F does not vanish on the complement [a, b ] \ [c, d ]. These three steps prove the existence of a unique root in [ a, b ], lying on [ c, d ], and are performed using the interval arithmetic implementations FInt and F ′

> Int

. In practice, we look for a sharp enclosure of the root, meaning that we prove the root lies in a very small subinterval [ c, d ], since this is useful for the stability CAPs. The case of nonexistence of roots is treated similarly. Once we have numerical evidence that F has no roots on [ a, b ], we compute FInt ([ a, b ]) and verify that this interval does not contain zero. The procedures described above may fail when the values of F or F ′ remains close to zero over a substantial portion of [ a, b ]. In such cases, the interval arithmetic implementations FInt and F ′ 

> Int

may be unable to determine the sign of F or F ′ on the relevant subintervals. Suppose now that the function F : [ a, b ] → R, like the functions f1, . . . , f 4 and gκ,μ , depends on the parameters ( κ, μ ). Our approach treats these parameters as intervals. Specifically, for given values of κ and 

μ, we consider the intervals [κ − δκ, κ + δκ] and [μ − δμ, μ + δμ],

where δκ, δ μ > 0. Each choice of ( κ, μ ) together with δκ and δμ determines a rectangle in the parameter plane. If the interval-arithmetic procedure described above successfully determines the existence and location of roots of F on [ a, b ], then the conclusion is valid on the whole parameter rectangle. Similar considerations allow us to treat the dependence of the endpoints of the interval [ a, b ] on the parameters ( κ, μ ). To obtain results on larger regions of parameter space, we cover the region of interest by a finite collection of overlapping parameter rectangles and perform the same verification on each rectangle. The procedure described above is not directly applicable to the functions fj , j = 1 , . . . , 4, because their domains Ij are open intervals and the functions become unbounded near the endpoints. To address this issue, we first define appropriate closed subintervals Jj ⊂ I j , and prove analytically that all roots of fj in 

Ij are contained in Jj . The details are presented in Sec. C.3 below.

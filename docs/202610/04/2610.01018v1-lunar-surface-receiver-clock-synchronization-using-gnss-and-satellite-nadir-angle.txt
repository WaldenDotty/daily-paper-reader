Abstract —The use of signals from Earth-orbiting Global Nav-igation Satellite System (GNSS) constellations for positioning, navigation, and timing on the lunar surface was recently demon-strated by the Lunar GNSS Receiver Experiment (LuGRE) aboard Blue Ghost Mission 1. For receiver clock synchronization using GNSS, Kalman filter performance depends on the satellite-specific measurement uncertainty used to construct the mea-surement noise covariance. Terrestrial measurement weighting commonly relies on receiver elevation angle, but the elevation angles in the analyzed LuGRE data are confined to 24.2 ◦–31.5 ◦, providing limited discrimination of measurement quality. In contrast, satellite nadir angles span 12.1 ◦–66.6 ◦, and the measurement variability increases substantially at small nadir angles. Based on this observation, we propose a measurement weighting model that combines satellite nadir angle with C/N 0

for lunar surface receiver clock synchronization. The proposed model is evaluated against three weighting methods adopted from the literature using seven LuGRE operation periods (OPs). It achieves the lowest receiver clock bias root mean square error (RMSE) in six of the seven OPs and an overall RMSE of 5.78 m, which is 19.5% lower than that of the best comparison model. The proposed model also provides the lowest clock bias prediction RMSE under simulated measurement outages. 

Index Terms —lunar PNT, clock synchronization, measurement noise modeling, satellite nadir angle, Kalman filter. 

I. I NTRODUCTION

Such signals can provide an additional source of position and timing information for lunar missions and complement dedicated lunar navigation infrastructure [15], [16]. The Lunar GNSS Receiver Experiment (LuGRE) aboard Blue Ghost Mission 1 (BGM1) [17] recently demonstrated this capability by receiving and processing Global Positioning System (GPS) and Galileo signals on the lunar surface [18]. Accurate receiver clock synchronization is an important component of PNT using GNSS, particularly when measure-ments are weak, intermittent, or geometrically constrained. Previous studies have also investigated clock synchronization and clock state prediction under limited GNSS availabil-ity [8], [19]. In a Kalman filter-based clock estimator, the measurement update depends on the uncertainty assigned to each satellite observation, which determines the measurement noise covariance used by the filter. Terrestrial GNSS stochas-tic models commonly characterize this uncertainty using re-ceiver elevation angle, carrier-to-noise density ratio ( C/N 0), or combinations of these quantities [20]–[22]. Elevation-based weighting is effective in many terrestrial environments because measurements received at low elevation angles are generally more susceptible to atmospheric effects and multipath [23]. The receiver elevation geometry is substantially different on the lunar surface. Because the Earth–Moon distance is much larger than the spatial extent of the GNSS constella-tions, Earth-orbiting GNSS satellites occupy a narrow angular region when viewed from a lunar receiver [10], [11], [16]. Here, the satellite nadir angle denotes the angle, measured at the transmitting GNSS satellite, between the satellite-to-Earth-center direction and the satellite-to-receiver direction. In the seven LuGRE operation periods (OPs) considered in this study, the receiver elevation angles were confined to 24.2 ◦–31.5 ◦, whereas the corresponding satellite nadir angles spanned 12.1 ◦–66.6 ◦. More importantly, the LuGRE mea-surement variability showed no clear systematic dependence over the narrow elevation angle range, whereas it increased substantially at small satellite nadir angles. Based on these observations, we propose a measure-ment weighting model that combines satellite nadir angle with C/N 0 for lunar surface receiver clock synchronization. The proposed model is evaluated using seven LuGRE OPs against three weighting methods adopted from the literature: equal weighting, C/N 0-based weighting, and elevation-based weighting. A. LuGRE Dataset and Reference Solution 

This study uses GPS L5 and Galileo E5a observations collected by the LuGRE payload on the lunar surface after the BGM1 landing [18]. Seven OPs are analyzed: OP38 0, OP40 0, OP74 0, OP76 0, OP77 0, OP77 1, and OP78 1. Their durations range from tens of minutes to more than 20 hours, providing a total of 400,821 satellite observations. Satellite positions were obtained from WHU precise orbit products [27], while satellite clock corrections and code biases were obtained from the associated WHU clock and observable-specific bias products [28]. For each observation, the satellite position was linearly interpolated to the signal transmission time, approximated as ttx = trx −pr raw /c . The corresponding satellite velocity used in (2) was obtained by finite differencing successive precise orbit positions at 1 s intervals. The lunar receiver position was generated from the known BGM1 landing site coordinates of 18 .5623 ◦N and 61 .8103 ◦E[33], expressed in the Moon-fixed IAU MOON frame. The site radius was set to 1733.75 km, corresponding to a site elevation of 3.650 km below the mean lunar radius of 1737.4 km. At each epoch, the landing site position was transformed to J2000 using the lunar orientation model [34]. The Moon’s geocentric position was obtained from the DE440s ephemeris [35], after which the resulting Earth-centered J2000 position was trans-formed to the International Terrestrial Reference Frame 1993 (ITRF93) Earth-fixed frame using NASA’s SPICE toolkit [36], [37]. The resulting epoch-dependent Earth-centered receiver position was used to compute the geometric range in (1) and the nadir angle in (7). For elevation angle computation, the receiver local vertical was defined with respect to the center of the Moon. Across the seven OPs, the elevation angles were confined to 24.2 ◦–31.5 ◦, with a mean of 27.9 ◦ and a standard deviation of 1.4 ◦. In contrast, the nadir angles spanned 12.1 ◦–66.6 ◦,with a mean of 21.2 ◦ and a standard deviation of 7.4 ◦.The substantially wider nadir angle range provides greater geometric variation for satellite-specific measurement noise modeling. To reduce direct information overlap between the estimated and reference clock solutions, the Kalman filter measurements in (1) are constructed using code phase observations only. The reference clock solution used for performance evaluation is reconstructed using both code and carrier phase observations. The carrier phase observations are segmented into continuous arcs, leveled to the code observations, and jointly adjusted, with the absolute clock level anchored by the code measure-ments. This separation reduces the risk that a measurement weighting scheme is favored simply because it resembles the combination procedure used to generate the reference solution.

C. Evaluation Procedure 

All four measurement noise models are applied to the same two-state clock Kalman filter described in Section II-A. The LuGRE receiver employs a voltage-controlled temperature-compensated crystal oscillator [40]. The process noise pa-rameters are computed from representative temperature-compensated crystal oscillator noise coefficients reported in [41], 

q21c = 0 .0090 m 2/s, q22c = 0 .0355 m 2/s3. (14) The same filter settings are used for all models, and mea-surement gating and filter reinitialization are disabled to main-tain identical comparison conditions. The filter is initialized at the first epoch of each OP with b0 = z0, ˙b0 = 0 , and a diffuse initial covariance of 10 8 m2 for bias and 10 4 (m/s) 2

for drift, reflecting the absence of prior information. Clock synchronization performance is evaluated in terms of the receiver clock bias RMSE for each OP and for the pooled dataset. For the proposed model, the per-OP RMSE values therefore also correspond to the held-out results of the leave-one-OP-out cross-validation. As a complementary evaluation, clock bias prediction un-der simulated measurement outages is performed to examine the quality of the estimated clock states after measurement updates are removed. Within each OP, outage start epochs are selected at intervals of 30 min, excluding the 60 s immediately following a signal gap. A total of 142 outage start epochs are evaluated across the seven OPs, with overlapping prediction windows treated independently. From each start epoch, the estimated bias and drift states are propagated for up to one hour without measurement updates and compared with the reference clock solution. Prediction errors are evaluated over five elapsed time intervals: 0–60 s, 60–300 s, 300–900 s, 900– 1800 s, and 1800–3600 s. IV. R ESULTS

The proposed model achieved the lowest receiver clock bias RMSE in six of the seven OPs as well as for the pooled dataset. The only exception was OP40 0, where the C/N 0-based model achieved 5.10 m compared with 5.16 m for the proposed model. The overall RMSE was 5.78 m, compared with 8.73 m for equal weighting, 7.18 m for C/N 0-based weighting, and 8.13 m for elevation-based weighting. Compared with the best comparison model ( C/N 0-based weighting), the proposed model reduced the overall RMSE by 19.5%. The per-OP RMSE variation is also consistent with dif-ferences in satellite availability and observation geometry. For example, OP76 0, which yields the largest RMSE under the proposed model, has substantially higher fractions of single-satellite epochs and small-nadir-angle observations than OP78 1, which yields the lowest RMSE. These results indicate that the proposed model provides more effective satellite-specific measurement weighting than the evaluated terrestrial weighting models under the LuGRE lunar surface geometry. 

B. Clock Bias Prediction Under Measurement Outages 

Table II summarizes the clock bias prediction RMSE as a function of elapsed time after the beginning of each simulated measurement outage. The proposed model achieved the lowest prediction RMSE in all five elapsed time intervals. The improvement was most pronounced immediately after the beginning of an outage. During the first 60 s, the proposed model achieved an RMSE of 12.78 m, which was 29.0% lower than the 18.00 m obtained with the best comparison model, the C/N 0-based method. Because the measurement noise covariance affects the Kalman filter only during measurement updates, its influence on the outage prediction is indirect. The different measurement noise models produce different bias and drift state estimates at the beginning of each outage, after which the states are propagated without further measurement correction. As the prediction horizon increases, the relative differences among the four models decrease substantially, indicating that long-term prediction error becomes increasingly dominated by clock state propagation rather than by the preceding measurement weighting. This behavior is consistent with the limitation of propagating the clock state under a constant drift model over long measurement outages [12]. V. C ONCLUSION 

This study developed a measurement weighting model that incorporates satellite nadir angle and C/N 0 for lunar surface receiver clock synchronization using GNSS. Analysis of seven LuGRE OPs showed that receiver elevation angle was confined to a narrow range and exhibited no clear systematic relation-ship with measurement variability. In contrast, measurement variability increased substantially at small satellite nadir an-gles. Based on this observation, the proposed model assigns satellite-specific measurement uncertainty using satellite nadir angle and C/N 0.The proposed model achieved the lowest receiver clock bias RMSE in six of the seven OPs. Its overall RMSE was 5.78 m, representing a 19.5% reduction relative to the best comparison model. The proposed model also achieved the lowest clock bias prediction RMSE in all evaluated intervals under simulated measurement outages, with the largest relative improvement observed during the first 60 s. These results indicate that satellite observation geometry can provide useful information for GNSS measurement weighting when receiver elevation angles are confined to a narrow range at lunar distances. Future work should further investigate the physical mechanisms underlying the increased measurement variability at small nadir angles. The proposed model should also be evaluated using additional lunar GNSS datasets and receiver configurations to assess the transferability of the selected parameters. ACKNOWLEDGMENT

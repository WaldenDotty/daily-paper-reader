# OrbitTAMP: Grounding Language Models for Task and Motion Planning in Spacecraft Rendezvous 

Yuji Takubo , Student Member, IEEE Stanford University, Stanford, CA 94305, USA 

Daniele Gammelli , Member, IEEE Stanford University, Stanford, CA 94305, USA Italian Institute of Artificial Intelligence (AI4I), Turin, Italy

Abstract— Spacecraft rendezvous and proximity operations (RPO) are currently planned through an expertise-intensive process in which engineers translate high-level operational intent into safe, dynamically feasible trajectories, creating a bottleneck to scalable operations. Large language model (LLM)-based agents could offer an intuitive interface for this process, although their outputs are not inherently grounded in orbital dynamics, operational constraints, or the structure of admissible spacecraft maneuvers. To exploit their semantic reasoning while ensuring the generated plan’s physical validity, this paper presents a hierarchical framework for spacecraft task-and-motion planning (TAMP) that grounds LLM reasoning in a graph of reusable behaviors and domain-specific planning modules. Within this framework, a pretrained LLM maps a natural-language command to a partial mission specification. The associated planners then resolve unspecified decisions within the admissible operational space. Finally, trajectory optimization converts the completed mission specification into a dynamically feasible tra-jectory. Numerical experiments demonstrate that this architecture substantially improves intent recovery over direct LLM generation, achieving 98% exact recovery of partial mission specifications across all evaluated splits when backed by frontier LLMs. Additional test-time-compute experiments show that, for a compact 9B model, verifier-guided revision increases exact recovery from 75% to 88%, while broader behavior-plan search independently improves selection among admissible trajectory realizations. Overall, these results establish a scalable and auditable foundation for language-driven agentic planning of spacecraft RPO.

I. Introduction 

The proliferation of distributed space systems, such as swarms and constellations, is increasing the need for scalable spacecraft operations. Rendezvous and proximity operations (RPO), in particular, require complex maneu-ver sequences for inspection, repair, refueling, and other servicing tasks. Traditionally, teams of operators and mission designers translate high-level mission intent or concepts of operations (CONOPS) into explicit trajectory specifications using their knowledge of astrodynamics, mission constraints, and available guidance modes. This dependence on specialized expertise limits the scalability of future spacecraft operations. Large language models (LLMs) have the potential to establish natural language as an intuitive and broadly accessible interface between high-level mission intent and executable spacecraft trajectories. Specifically, recent advances in agentic LLM frameworks have demonstrated that foundation models (FMs) can interpret high-level instructions, decompose objectives into multistep plans, invoke external tools, and coordinate reasoning with action across interactive decision-making, autonomous scientific experimentation, and embodied operations [1], [2], [3]. For spacecraft RPO, however, semantic interpre-tation alone is insufficient. Language-model outputs must be grounded in spacecraft-specific behavior structures, operational constraints, and orbital dynamics before they can support safe execution. The central challenge is there-fore not merely to translate language into a quantitative configuration, but to embed LLM reasoning within a planning architecture that converts incomplete intent into admissible and physically realizable spacecraft motion. Consider the command: “ approach the target slowly from the −V -bar, circumnavigate it for two orbits, and then depart to a 50 m along-track separation. ” To a hu-man operator, this command conveys a clear maneuver in-tent through an ordered composition of familiar spacecraft behaviors: approach, circumnavigate, and depart. Such reusable behaviors provide abstractions for organizing complex motion, similar in function to skills [3] or meta-actions [4] in broader robotic systems. As a trajectory-planning specification, however, the command remains in-complete. It dictates several explicit requirements, includ-ing the two-orbit circumnavigation and final separation, while leaving other decisions such as phase durations, intermediate waypoints, and trajectory constraints unspec-ified. This distinction is central to spacecraft trajectory planning: conventional task and trajectory planners typi-cally operate on formally defined actions, objectives, and constraints, whereas natural-language mission commands generally specify only a subset of this information, and the unstated remainder is where mission-design expertise must be applied. Grounding such a command therefore requires identifying the stated intent, resolving unspeci-fied decisions within the admissible spacecraft operational space, and constructing a dynamically feasible and safe trajectory.

The intent parsing initializes the partial mission specification M0;behavior sequencing produces M1; waypoint generation produces M2; and trajectory optimization generates the final state and control trajectories. All three specifications are successive states of the same MissionConfig .To address this problem, this paper introduces a hi-erarchical framework for grounding LLM reasoning for spacecraft task-and-motion planning (TAMP), illustrated in Fig. 1. Extending the preliminary concepts of structural decomposition for spacecraft behavior planning [5], [6], [7], the framework organizes the planning process into three stages: semantic grounding, completion of unspec-ified mission decisions, and physical trajectory verifica-tion. A pretrained LLM first maps the natural-language command to a partial mission specification that preserves the operator’s stated intent while explicitly represent-ing unspecified information. A graph of reusable space-craft behaviors, together with domain-specific planning modules, then completes the behavior sequence, timing, and waypoint decisions within the admissible operational space. Finally, trajectory optimization instantiates the re-sulting candidates as state-control trajectories and ensures their dynamical feasibility and safety. This hierarchical architecture grounds LLM reasoning at each stage while enabling multistage planning, computational tool use, and intermediate verification. The central contribution of this paper is a hierarchical planning formulation in which operator underspecification is preserved and systematically completed, realized as an agentic architecture that scaffolds pretrained, off-the-shelf LLMs with structured, spacecraft-specific planning and verification modules. Because every stage completes the same partial specification, stated intent is preserved by construction rather than by prompt compliance and remains checkable throughout, while unstated decisions are resolved by the behavior graph and planning modules under orbital dynamics and safety constraints rather than left to the LLM. This design is motivated by both the scarcity of domain-specific training data for space appli-cations [8] and the need for transparent, verifiable, and explicitly constrained decision-making in safety-critical spacecraft operations. The resulting architecture provides an auditable human–spacecraft interface and supports scalable language-driven planning for future distributed space systems. Specifically, the contributions of this work are as follows: 

• A spacecraft RPO planning representation compris-ing a graph of reusable behaviors and a structured mission configuration that preserves partial operator specifications and supports hierarchical completion of behavior, timing, and waypoint decisions. 

• An LLM-backed TAMP formulation that uses these representations to decouple intent parsing from tra-jectory realization, grounding semantic decisions in the admissible behavior structure and resolving un-stated decisions within operational constraints and spacecraft dynamics. 

• A stage-wise and end-to-end evaluation over 300 procedurally generated commands and multiple LLM backends and a deterministic parsing baseline that quantifies the effects of the grounding scaffold, model capability, and targeted test-time computation through verifier-guided revision and behavior-plan search. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Sec. II compares the proposed framework with spacecraft planning, language grounding, and robotic TAMP. Sec. III defines the common mission representation, behavior graph, and systematic completion process. Sec. IV de-scribes the on-orbit inspection case study and its specific implementations. Sec. V reports the module-level and end-to-end evaluation results. Finally, Sec. VI concludes the paper and discusses future work. 

II. Related Work 

This work builds on two closely related lines of research: spacecraft planning and trajectory generation, and language-grounded TAMP for robotic systems. The following review for each line of research is provided below. 

A. Spacecraft Planning, Language Grounding, and Trajectory Optimization 

Classical spacecraft autonomy separates high-level mission planning from guidance, navigation, and control.

Onboard systems have demonstrated activity planning, scheduling, execution monitoring, and replanning from predefined goals [9], [10]. Autonomous decision-making based on discrete logic has also been developed for formation flying and rendezvous and proximity operations [5], [11], [12]. In these systems, expert-defined activity models and transition logic specify high-level tasking and behavior sequencing, while waypoint and maneuver generation is generally handled by separate modules. Trajectory optimization provides a principled mecha-nism for generating a state-control trajectory that enforces the underlying dynamics, waypoint conditions, and opera-tional constraints [13], [14]. Learning-based methods can improve its numerical performance through warm-starting or approximation [15], [16], [17], [18]. These methods generally assume that the maneuver structure, waypoints, objectives, and constraints have already been specified based on the desired high-level spacecraft behaviors. Recent work has explored the use of semantic rea-soning for spacecraft operations [19], [8], [20]. Parallel research translates natural-language safety requirements into mathematical representations, including explicit con-straints and signal temporal logic formulas [21], [22]. Prior work by the authors learned language-conditioned trajectory warm starts from paired command–trajectory data and refined them through sequential convex program-ming (SCP) [23]. Collectively, these approaches provide complementary capabilities for high-level spacecraft autonomy and con-tinuous trajectory generation with potential for connection with semantics. Their interface nevertheless remains de-pendent on expert-specified maneuver definitions or com-plete trajectory-level representations, leaving no struc-tured mechanism for preserving partial operator intent, resolving unspecified mission decisions, and verifying the resulting behavior sequence under orbital dynamics and safety constraints, in a scalable manner. 

B. Learning-Guided Task and Motion Planning 

TAMP integrates discrete task-level decision-making with continuous motion generation [24]. Classical TAMP evaluates symbolic choices through motion-level sam-pling or optimization [25], [26], whereas learning-guided approaches leverage data to prioritize task decisions, propose continuous parameters, or filter infeasible can-didates [27], [28], [29]. These methods provide a general framework for coupling behavior selection with trajectory generation. Language-conditioned planning in robotics provides a complementary perspective by enabling high-level in-structions to guide planning and control. Existing ap-proaches combine the reasoning capabilities of language models with learned affordances, executable programs, or external planners [3], [30], [31]. Some use LLM to propose or prioritize skill-level plans under feasibility models [32], while others translate instructions into a structured formal task specifications used by downstream planners [33], [34]. Collectively, these methods demonstrate how language-level reasoning can be connected to executable motion through structured planning abstractions. However, their formulations generally seek a sufficiently specified symbolic problem or skill sequence and do not address the spacecraft-specific need to preserve operator underspecification while completing behavior, timing, and waypoint decisions within an admissible decision space for RPO missions. Taken together, prior work in spacecraft mission planning, trajectory optimization, language grounding, and robotic TAMP provides complementary pieces to-wards language-driven spacecraft operations. The pro-posed framework unifies these directions by representing language as a partial mission specification and system-atically completing its unspecified decisions through a graph of admissible spacecraft behaviors, trajectory-aware evaluation, and final trajectory optimization. 

III. Methodology 

The objective of this work is to generate an executable spacecraft trajectory from an operator’s high-level intent, expressed as a natural-language command. The planner must first infer an admissible sequence of spacecraft behaviors and then solve for the corresponding continuous trajectory. Because natural-language commands may omit relevant details or leave them underspecified, the frame-work must also identify and resolve missing information while preserving the operator’s stated intent. The overall architecture is summarized in Fig. 1. The framework takes as input a natural-language command and a mission scenario specifying the initial relative state, default constraints, and other mission parameters. A shared representation, MissionConfig , carries the evolving mission specification throughout the pipeline. First, the intent parser maps the command to a partial specification, M0. The behavior-duration planning stage then uses the behavior graph to complete the behavior sequence and phase durations, yielding M1. Next, the waypoint planning stage completes the corresponding waypoint specifications to produce M2. Finally, trajectory optimization instantiates M2 as state–control trajectories subject to the adopted dynamics and safety constraints. Thus, M0, M1, and M2 represent successive stages in the completion of a single MissionConfig , with operator-specified information preserved throughout the pipeline. The following subsection introduces the shared plan-ning representations that support the proposed hierarchy. The planning modules are then described in sequence to show how these representations structure the admissible search space while preserving operator intent and ensuring that the resulting trajectory satisfies the adopted safety and dynamical feasibility requirements.

A. Common Planning Representation 

To make this planning problem tractable, this pa-per introduces two complementary abstractions: a graph-based representation of reusable spacecraft behaviors and a structured representation of the mission specification. 

1. Behavior–Waypoint Graph 

First, the trajectory is assumed to decompose into a sequence of behavior primitives , each representing an admissible transfer between prescribed waypoint domains over an allowable range of transfer times. Namely, this paper assumes that a trajectory can be represented as a sequence of waypoint-hopping transfers. This abstraction yields the behavior–waypoint graph G(D, E), illustrated in Fig. 2, where D denotes the set of waypoint-domain nodes and E ⊆ D × B × D denotes the set of behavior-labeled edges, with B representing the set of behavior labels. Unlike previous works on kinodynamic motion-planning graphs [35], [36], the proposed behavior–waypoint graph operates at the CONOPS-level, representing continuous waypoint domains and admissible behavior transitions rather than discretized states or motion primitives. 

2. Mission Configuration Interface 

The second key abstraction is MissionConfig ,a structured representation of the intermediate mission specification shared between the operator-facing interface and downstream planning modules. B. Hierarchical Planning 

With the two planning representations established, the following subsections describe each stage of the planning pipeline. The behavior-duration planning and waypoint generation modules expose common comple-tion interfaces without prescribing a particular inference mechanism; therefore, each may be implemented using deterministic or learning-based methods. Regardless of the implementation, previously populated fields are pre-served, and a completed mission specification is accepted only after its dynamical feasibility and safety have been verified through trajectory optimization. 

1. Intent Parsing 

The first module, the intent parser, maps the semantics of the input command to an initial MissionConfig .Using a curated prompt, a pretrained LLM translates the command into a structured JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) representation of the relevant mission semantics,

2. Behavior-Duration Sequence Planning 

The behavior-duration planner determines the se-quence of spacecraft behaviors to be executed and the duration of each behavior. First, the planner enumerates all graph-valid behavior–and-duration plans consistent with the information specified in M0. Graph validity alone, however, does not guarantee that a candidate admits a feasible or desirable trajectory. Although each candidate could, in principle, be evaluated by completing its waypoint specification and solving the corresponding trajectory optimization problem, repeating this process for every candidate may be computationally prohibitive. IV. Case Study: On-Orbit Inspection in Low Earth Orbit 

The proposed architecture is demonstrated in an on-orbit inspection scenario in low Earth orbit, where a servicer spacecraft approaches a target spacecraft and performs close-proximity operations. Consistent with the hierarchical validation strategy, the case study presents the modules from downstream to upstream: trajectory optimization, waypoint generation and behavior-duration planning, and, finally, intent parsing. This ordering al-lows each lower-level module to be established before evaluating its upstream dependencies and, ultimately, the end-to-end pipeline. The case study considers behavior sequences com-prising two or three phases, such that M ∈ 2, 3. D. Behavior-Duration Planning and Waypoint Generation 

The case study considers deterministic and learning-based realizations of the two planning modules introduced in Sec. III. For behavior-duration planning, candidate quality is obtained either from an optimization-derived leg library or from a learned metric surrogate Qψ . For waypoint generation, continuous states are selected using either a representative-waypoint heuristic or a learned policy pϕ. The following subsections describe these re-alizations. 

1. Coupled Behavior–Waypoint Realization 

A simple heuristic selects representative waypoints at the midpoint of each admissible domain in Table I, despite its suboptimality. This reduces the problem to joining pre-computed legs between representative waypoints, making candidate screening and ranking fast. A representative waypoint is placed at the midpoint of each connected component of the E/I-separated domains (i.e., E0, E+,and E−), and at the midpoint of each flat domain (i.e., 

F+ and F−). An optimization-derived leg library is constructed offline by solving Eq. (11) for every admissible transfer between representative waypoints, duration grid, target semimajor axis, and KOZ radius considered in the case study. Each library entry records whether the transfer converges and its associated trajectory-quality metrics. In particular, the library assumes a circular Keplerian orbit (e = 0 and no J2 perturbations), reducing the target’s orbit parameterization to its semimajor axis instead of the full six-dimensional state. Note that this assumption could induce deployment error. However, this approximation keeps the library compact, with only 2,160 entries across the three KOZ radii at the nominal semimajor axis. At deployment, each behavior–duration candidate in 

C(M0, s ) is expanded over its admissible representative-waypoint chains. Because the measured initial state gener-ally does not coincide with a representative waypoint, the first leg of each chain, from the current state to the first  

> TAKUBO ET AL.: OrbitTAMP: Grounding Language Models for Task and Motion Planning in Spacecraft Rendezvous 7

representative waypoint, is solved online using SCP. All subsequent legs are retrieved from the offline leg library. A chain is retained only if the online first-leg solve and all required library entries are feasible. The correspond-ing leg-level metrics are then composed to rank them: fuel consumption, flight time, and effective observation exposure are summed across the legs, whereas the safety margin is given by the minimum leg-level value. This composition provides an auditable mechanism for candidate screening and ranking. It is distinct from the final full-horizon trajectory optimization, which receives the selected waypoint sequence and generates the final verified trajectory. 

2. Heuristic Waypoint Generation 

The heuristic policy constructs the waypoint sequence incrementally based on the same representative waypoint set used in the coupled behavior–waypoint realization. For an E/I-separated destination domain, the heuristic selects the connected component whose midpoint is closest to the preceding reference state, whereas the along-track coordinate is placed at the midpoint of the destination interval; this preserves the sign combination of (δe y , δi y )

at the previous waypoint. Flat-domain waypoints are placed at their domain centers. 

3. Learning-Based Waypoint Generation 

The learning-based waypoint policy is the conditional model pϕ(w1: M | s, b1: M , d1: M ), where ϕ denotes the model parameters. The model is supervised using the optimization-derived converged trajectories. The output parameterization of the model is carefully designed so that it guarantees that decoded waypoints remain within the graph-prescribed waypoint domains, even for do-mains that support disconnected subdomains, such as E/I-separated domains (i.e., E0, E+, and E−). Details on network architecture, parameterization, and loss function are provided in the Supplementary Material Sec. A. 

4. Learning-Based Behavior-Duration Planning 

Learning-based behavior-duration planning uses a sur-rogate model Qψ , with parameters ψ, to predict the convergence of trajectory optimization and metric out-comes of a candidate behavior-duration sequence. The training dataset is generated by randomizing the scenario and behavior-duration sequence, applying a specified waypoint-generation policy πw, and solving the trajec-tory optimization. Although flight time is a deterministic function of the behavior-duration sequence in this case study, the surrogate predicts all four preference metrics for the uniform evaluation of the metric-prediction interface. At deployment, graph-valid candidates are evaluated in a batched forward pass, candidates below the convergence threshold are removed, and the remainder are ranked using the same deterministic preference map ρP to de-termine the top-ranked behavior-duration plan. Details on the architecture, parameterization, and loss function are provided in the Supplementary Material Sec. B. 

E. Intent Parsing 

The intent parser uses a pretrained off-the-shelf LLM to map an operator command into the partial mission specification M0 as a structured output. The prompt pro-vides the behavior–waypoint ontology and output schema, and instructs the model to populate only information explicitly supported by the command, leaving unspecified fields at ⊥. Because populated entries are preserved by downstream planning stages, this conservative parsing rule avoids prematurely completing the mission. Details on the prompt development are given in the Supplemen-tary Material Sec. C. A central challenge in evaluating intent parsing is establishing an unambiguous reference for the opera-tor’s intended M0. Evaluation commands are therefore generated label-first : a mission intent and corresponding reference specification M⋆ 

> 0

are constructed, and then the command is rendered systematically using family-specific templates. No language model participates in either label construction or command generation, providing an exactly known reference and avoiding circular evaluation. The sampled mission families span maneuver composition, quantitative constraints, preferences, and expert overrides. Based on the label-first command generation, three 100-command splits are developed: development , held-out instance , and held-out structural to develop the structured prompts for the LLM and to evaluate the intent parsing capability in the following section. The development split is used for prompt development and contains commands rendered using the nominal family-specific linguistic templates. The held-out-instance split contains independently sampled mission specifications and behavior compositions expressed using the same linguistic styles as the development split, thereby eval-uating generalization to unseen mission instances without introducing an additional language-distribution shift. The held-out-structural split similarly remains within the sup-ported mission families and behavior–waypoint ontology, but additionally renders the commands using an unseen terse radio-communication register. This split therefore evaluates sensitivity to changes in linguistic structure while preserving the underlying planning vocabulary and mission semantics. The details of the commands’ linguis-tic styles are summarized in the Supplementary Material Sec. D. At deployment, the parser may abstain when no valid specification is obtained. A graph-validity guard rejects behavior–waypoint combinations inconsistent with the graph before downstream planning. 

V. Results 

This section evaluates the proposed framework along three axes: architectural scaffolding, model capability, and test-time computation. Each axis corresponds to a design decision that arises when deploying a natural-language    

> 8IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON AEROSPACE AND ELECTRONIC SYSTEMS VOL. XX, No. XX XXXXX 2020

interface for spacecraft operations and therefore warrants evaluation beyond the architecture itself. The first axis concerns how domain knowledge is incorporated into the system. Because orbital dynam-ics, operational constraints, and mission-design conven-tions may not be reliably encoded within a general-purpose LLM, the proposed framework externalizes this knowledge through a structured mission representation, a behavior–waypoint ontology, and dedicated planning modules. Thus, the distinct contributions of these com-ponents and of the resulting hierarchical decomposition must be evaluated separately. The second axis concerns the capability of the underlying language model. Candidate backends range from compact models suitable for onboard or resource-constrained deployment to frontier models intended pri-marily for ground-based planning. Accordingly, the evalu-ation must consider not only overall performance but also how the nature of failure changes with model capability, including the violation of the structured interface and misinterpretation of the intent. The third axis concerns the allocation of computation at inference time. Additional test-time computation de-voted to reasoning, verification, or search has been shown to substantially improve performance without requiring model retraining [42]. Within the proposed hierarchy, this computation may be assigned to semantic refinement during intent parsing or candidate search during behavior planning. As they address different failure modes, whether their gains are complementary determines how a fixed computational budget should be distributed across the pipeline. Together, these axes motivate the following research questions, which are evaluated at the level of both the individual planning modules and the complete language-to-trajectory pipeline: 1

1) RQ1 (Architecture) : What are the distinct contri-butions of architectural scaffolding and hierarchi-cal planning to language-to-trajectory generation? 2) RQ2 (LLM Capability) : How does LLM capa-bility affect structured intent recognition, failure modes, and end-to-end mission satisfaction? 3) RQ3 (Test-time Compute) : How does the addi-tional test-time computation across the planning hierarchy affect intent reliability and realized tra-jectory preference?

D. RQ3: Test-Time Compute Across the Planning Hierarchy 

The results in RQ2 show that the frontier LLM approaches the ceiling of the semantic understanding of the proposed framework, whereas smaller models retain a non-negligible error rate in intent parsing. The final RQ therefore investigates whether additional allocation of test-time computation can improve performance without retraining or increasing the baseline model size, and how its effect depends on where that computation is allocated within the planning hierarchy. In this subsection, Qwen3.5-9B is used as a representative compact model, and the 100-command development split is used for the natural-language command data. Test-time compute is considered at two levels of the hierarchy: computation at intent parsing through verifier-guided revision and multi-hypothesis generation, and computation at down-stream behavior planning through evaluation of additional behavior–duration candidates.

2. Behavior-Duration Planning 

A second axis of test-time computation expands the downstream search over behavior–duration plans for a fixed parsed specification M0. Unlike the semantic mech-anisms above, this search does not improve intent align-ment; instead, it seeks a more preferable trajectory among admissible realizations of the recovered mission spec-ification. For each M0, graph-valid behavior–duration candidates are first ranked by the surrogate Qψ as de-scribed in Sec. IV-D4. The top Nplan candidates are then instantiated using the minimum-displacement waypoint heuristic and evaluated through the trajectory optimization under the variable-waypoint contract. Among the suc-cessfully optimized candidates, the final plan is selected according to the realized lexicographic preference. Thus, increasing Nplan expends additional compute in trajectory optimization rather than additional LLM calls. Figure 8 evaluates this planning-search budget relative to the nominal Nplan = 1 solution. Because the candidate sets are nested and the best successfully optimized tra-jectory is retained, increasing Nplan cannot degrade the realized preference relative to Nplan = 1 . Increasing the search budget progressively improves trajectory quality: evaluating the top five candidates yields a preferred so-lution for 34% ( Nrev = 0) and 43% ( Nrev = 2) of commands relative to using only the top-ranked candidate. The improvement persists under both revision budgets, indicating that verifier-guided semantic refinement and downstream planning search act on largely distinct aspects of the pipeline. Together with Fig. 3, these results show that Qψ provides an effective but coarse filter for promis-ing behavior–duration candidates, while full trajectory optimization of multiple top-ranked candidates can correct residual surrogate-ranking errors and improve the final realized preference. This benefit is therefore complemen-tary to semantic test-time computation, where revision im-proves the probability of recovering the intended mission specification, whereas behavior-planning search improves trajectory quality after semantic grounding. 

VI. Conclusion 

This paper introduced a hierarchical TAMP architec-ture that grounds LLM reasoning in spacecraft-specific behavior abstractions, operational constraints, and orbital dynamics to enable language-commanded trajectory gen-eration for rendezvous and proximity operations. Through a structured mission representation, a graph of reusable spacecraft behaviors, and domain-specific planning mod-ules, the architecture progressively translates high-level operator intent into a complete, verifiable mission speci-fication and, ultimately, a trajectory satisfying the adopted dynamics and safety constraints. This hierarchy sepa-rates semantic grounding, completion of unspecified mis-sion decisions, and physical trajectory verification while preserving operator-provided information throughout the planning process. Numerical experiments demonstrated the advantages of the hierarchical architecture over one-shot LLM-based planning, characterized how structured intent recognition and end-to-end mission satisfaction vary with model capability, and showed how test-time computation at different layers can improve semantic reliability and realized trajectory quality. Together, these results establish the proposed framework as a step toward scalable, auditable human–spacecraft interfaces for future agentic spacecraft operations. Future work will extend the framework to multi-spacecraft coordination and closed-loop execution with online replanning.

At every revision budget, a small deterministic check also compares the proposed M0 with certain movement sequences explicitly stated in the command. It applies only to (i) an ordered list of waypoint regions, (ii) an approach followed by departure, or (iii) a direct cross-side flyby or fast passage. Expressions such as F+, E0, F−, and their V -bar equivalents are matched to the waypoint regions in Table I, and the behavior–waypoint graph identifies the behavior associated with each stated movement. For example, F+ →

E0 → F− means that the first waypoint is in E0 and the second is in F−; F+ is the initial region and is therefore not repeated as a waypoint. At Nrev = 0 , any disagreement causes rejection; at Nrev > 0, it is returned to the LLM for revision. Commands outside these three cases receive no movement-specific feedback, and unrelated fields may remain at ⊥.After intent parsing, a final check determines whether ev-ery populated behavior can connect the preceding waypoint region to the requested next region in the behavior–waypoint graph, beginning from the known initial region. If a behavior is inconsistent but the requested waypoint remains reachable through another allowed behavior, that behavior entry is reset to ⊥, and the waypoint is preserved. This check does not fill unspecified fields or complete the behavior sequence.

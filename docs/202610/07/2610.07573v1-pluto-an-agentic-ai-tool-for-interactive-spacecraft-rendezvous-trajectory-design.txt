Abstract— Modern space missions require trajectory design methods capable of efficiently adapting to diverse mission objectives and op-erational constraints. Despite the maturity of non-convex trajectory optimization techniques, their broader adoption remains limited by the substantial domain expertise required to tailor problem formula-tions across varying mission scenarios. Recent advances in large lan-guage model (LLM) reasoning and agentic AI coding capabilities of-fer a promising pathway to improving the accessibility of advanced trajectory optimization techniques. This paper introduces PLUTO 

(Plain Language Understanding for Trajectory Optimization), an interactive trajectory design framework that integrates agentic AI coding tools within a sequential convex programming (SCP) archi-tecture. The proposed system maps high-level semantic inputs to well-defined mathematical constraints while preserving a structured representation suitable for SCP. These constraints are processed through an auto-convexification pipeline and incorporated directly into executable optimal control formulations, thereby systematically connecting user intent and trajectory generation. Visual constraint verification and scenario parameterization further support an in-tuitive environment for trajectory design across a wide range of application scenarios. Within an evaluation of 50 natural-language rendezvous mission prompts spanning 10 constraint types, PLUTO generated a trajectory for every prompt, and 94% of the resulting tra-jectories satisfied both the quantitative and qualitative requirements of the mission intent. Ultimately, this work explores the potential of agentic AI to bridge the gap between advanced optimization algorithms and engineer-focused trajectory design workflows. 

# 1. I NTRODUCTION 

Agentic artificial intelligence (AI) is creating new oppor-tunities for automating complex scientific and engineering workflows. Built on large multimodal models trained on internet-scale data, these systems combine reasoning and code generation with tool use, memory, and iterative feedback to carry out multistep tasks. Emerging applications across a range of disciplines demonstrate their ability to coordinate specialized software, incorporate domain knowledge, and use computational results to guide subsequent actions across a broad range of tasks [1], [2]. These capabilities are par-ticularly promising for engineering design, where translat-ing high-level objectives into computational models often requires substantial expertise and repeated iteration. Spacecraft trajectory design places heavy demands on this expertise. Mission-level objectives and operational require-ments must be translated into mathematical formulations that can be solved and validated computationally. # 2. R ELATED WORK 

The proposed framework builds upon prior advances in numerical trajectory optimization, learning-based trajectory generation, and LLM-based approaches for synthesizing mathematical constraints from natural-language specifica-tions. This section provides a brief overview of these research areas and summarizes the key limitations that motivate the proposed approach. 

Spacecraft Trajectory Optimization and Learning-Based Tra-jectory Generation 

Trajectory optimization serves as a foundational tool for both onboard guidance and the offline design of complex space missions, as it enables the simultaneous treatment of formal constraint satisfaction and control-cost optimality. To address the non-convexity arising from nonlinear dynamics and operational constraints, a variety of optimization meth-ods have been developed, including SCP [3], together with theoretical analyses establishing convergence properties and guarantees [4], [5], [6]. Because the performance of non-convex trajectory optimization can remain highly sensitive to the initial guess, intelligent warm-starting strategies have also been investigated, including learning-based approaches for generating high-quality initial trajectories [7], [8], [9], [10]. More recently, with the emergence of large language models (LLMs), their integration into spacecraft guidance, naviga-tion, and control architectures has received increasing atten-tion. Some studies investigate LLMs for direct reasoning and decision-making [11], [12], [13], while others consider language-conditioned trajectory generation for warm-starting SCP [14] or scaffolded LLM frameworks that systematically construct mission configurations within a predefined design space before invoking trajectory optimization [15]. A related tooling-based agentic framework for spacecraft formation control is presented in [16]. However, these approaches largely operate over fixed optimization formulations, with task-specific constraints either predefined by the system de-signer or not explicitly incorporated into the optimization problem. This leaves a key gap between natural-language mission intent and formal trajectory-optimization models. Our work addresses this gap by enabling zero-shot synthesis of mathe-matical constraints from natural-language specifications and incorporating those constraints directly into the optimization problem. In this way, operator intent can modify the formula-tion at the constraint level, rather than only selecting among predefined configurations or providing inputs to an otherwise fixed optimizer. 

Large Language Models for Constrained Optimization 

Recent work in optimization and operations research has investigated the translation of natural-language descriptions into structured mathematical formulations [17]. Building on this direction, agentic systems have been developed to construct optimization problems, generate executable solver code, and iteratively refine combinatorial optimization for-mulations [2]. Related efforts have addressed the translation of language into temporal-logic specifications [1], [18], [19], the enforcement of language-specified constraints through robotic safety mechanisms [20], [21], and the automated reformulation of non-convex optimization problems [22]. More closely related to optimal control, NARRATE combines sequential task planning with the generation of objective and constraint functions for model predictive control [23]. Building on these developments and advances in language-driven code generation [24], this work brings constraint synthesis directly into an SCP-based spacecraft trajectory optimization workflow. The agent generates executable, automatically differentiable constraint functions that can be incorporated into the optimization problem and, when non-convex, linearized within successive convex subproblems. This closes the loop between natural-language mission intent and numerical spacecraft trajectory optimization, enabling task-specific operational constraints to be directly injected into the optimization formulation itself rather than being limited by a predefined constraint set.

# 4. M ETHODOLOGY 

PLUTO operationalizes the language-to-optimization map-ping introduced in the previous section through four layers: a user interface, an agent scaffold, a numerical optimization backend, and a visualization and verification layer. The layers communicate through structured interfaces so that the agent is responsible for interpreting mission intent and constructing problem-specific artifacts, while the underlying numerical optimization routines remain explicitly defined in code. This separation allows the agent to modify the trajectory optimiza-tion formulation without requiring it to implement or alter the solver itself. At a high level, given a natural-language specification p and scenario context S, the agent produces a structured set of mathematical constraints and associated verification artifacts. The constraints are passed to the optimization backend, in-corporated into the OCP, and solved using SCP. The resulting trajectory and constraint evaluations are then exposed to the user for inspection and subsequent iteration. The following sections describe each layer and the interfaces between them. 

User Layer 

The user interacts with PLUTO through an agentic coding environment using a natural-language description of the de-sired spacecraft behavior. The request may define a new trajectory design scenario or modify an existing problem without requiring the user to provide the corresponding mathematical constraints, convexity classifications, or solver implementations. A PLUTO workspace contains the executable trajectory op-timization code together with the contextual information re-quired by the agent. The initial user request directs the agent to a workflow controller, which serves as the entry point to the framework and specifies how the remaining resources and tools should be used. In this way, the user-facing interface remains language-based while the downstream artifacts con-form to the structured interfaces expected by the optimization backend. 

Agent Layer 

The agent layer translates the user request into an executable representation of the trajectory optimization problem. Rather than allowing the agent to operate directly on the numerical solver without structure, PLUTO provides a domain-specific scaffold that constrains the workflow while retaining flexi-bility in how individual mission requirements are interpreted and formulated. 

Workflow Controller. The workflow controller defines the high-level sequence of operations the agent must perform and the artifacts that must be produced before execution is complete. It specifies guardrails on agent behavior, identifies the available modes of operation, and directs the agent to the domain-specific resources relevant to each stage of the work-flow. The controller therefore provides a common execution structure across agentic coding models while leaving the un-derlying model responsible for reasoning over the particular user request. 

Domain Knowledge and Reference Material. The work-flow controller provides access to three complementary forms of reference material. Templates define the structure of files and objects generated by the agent, ensuring compatibility with downstream interfaces. Scenario documents describe the relevant dynamics, geometry, coordinate conventions, and 3state representations. Finally, formulation guides provide instructions for decisions that require domain expertise, in-cluding the construction and numerical implementation of optimization constraints. Together, these resources constitute the scenario context S

introduced in the problem statement. They allow the agent to ground natural-language requirements in the conventions of the trajectory design problem rather than relying only on the pretrained model’s internal knowledge. 

Constraint Synthesis. For each mission requirement identi-fied in the user request, the agent determines an appropriate mathematical representation and the portion of the trajectory over which it should be enforced. The resulting constraint is encoded directly as a structured Python object containing its mathematical implementation, activation set, and convexity classification. The generated constraint objects are collected in a

ConstraintSet , which provides the primary interface between the agent and optimization layers. The agent ad-ditionally generates plotting and verification configurations associated with the synthesized requirements. These arti-facts preserve the connection between the original semantic request, its mathematical formulation, and the resulting tra-jectory. 

Optimization Layer 

PLUTO uses SCP as its numerical optimization backbone. This choice provides a common interface for incorporating a broad range of trajectory constraints while retaining the computational structure of convex optimization at each iter-ation. CVXPY is used to construct the convex subproblems, while JAX provides automatic differentiation for supported non-convex constraint functions. The optimization layer receives the agent-generated 

ConstraintSet and separates its members according to convexity. Constraints represented directly in CVXPY are incorporated into the corresponding convex subproblem with-out further approximation. Supported non-convex constraints represented in JAX are instead passed through the auto-convexification pipeline shown in Fig. 2. For a differentiable non-convex constraint: 

c(z) ≤ 0, (3) where z collects the relevant state and control variables, a first-order approximation about the current SCP reference point ¯z is given by: 

c(¯ z) + ∇c(¯ z)⊤ (z − ¯z) ≤ 0. (4) The required derivatives are obtained automatically using JAX. The resulting affine representation is converted into a CVXPY-compatible form and incorporated into the cur-rent convex subproblem. As the SCP iterations progress, supported non-convex constraints are re-linearized about the updated reference trajectory. 

Visualization and Verification Layer 

The final layer exposes the optimized trajectory and asso-ciated constraint information for analysis and user review. For each synthesized requirement, the agent generates a plotting or verification configuration that is processed by a common visualization pipeline. This allows scenario-specific

The resulting dashboard presents the spacecraft trajectory together with the active constraint regions and relevant optimization diagnostics. Numerical verification evaluates whether the generated trajectory satisfies the implemented constraints over their prescribed activation sets. Complemen-tary semantic verification allows the user to inspect whether the resulting trajectory and displayed constraint geometry are consistent with the original natural-language request. This distinction is important because a numerically satisfied con-straint may nevertheless reflect an incorrect interpretation of the user’s intent. The dashboard is generated as an HTML artifact that records the outputs associated with each scenario. By retaining the original request, generated formulation, optimized trajectory, and verification outputs within a common workflow, PLUTO supports iterative trajectory design in which the user can in-spect the current solution and refine the mission specification in subsequent interactions.

Experimental Results 

Overall, PLUTO performed well across all metrics. Although some runs showed deviations in Constraint Formulation or terminated non-optimally, these did not prevent the tool from producing trajectories that satisfied the prompt in most cases. This indicates that PLUTO and its underlying optimization framework are robust to imperfections at intermediate stages of the pipeline, allowing the tool to succeed even when individual layers are challenged. The distribution of metric results across the sets of one-, two-, and three-constraint prompts is shown in Table 2. Solver Completion was achieved for all 50 prompts, with no runs failing or reporting infeasibility, so a trajectory was available for evaluation in every case. Constraint Formulation matched the reference in 90% of prompts overall, decreasing slightly with complexity from 28 of 30 single-constraint prompts to 9 of 10 two-constraint and 8 of 10 three-constraint prompts. Optimal Convergence was the least consistent metric at 84% overall. Single-constraint prompts converged optimally in 28 of 30 cases and three-constraint prompts in 8 of 10, while two-constraint prompts dropped to 6 of 10, indicating that non-optimal convergence did not scale directly with the number of constraints. It should be noted that outside of the T10 constraint type, all single constraint scenarios did solve optimally, suggesting that at a core level individual basic constraints do not cause convergence issues. Despite these variations, Prompt Verification remained high across all levels of complexity, with 29 of 30 single-constraint prompts and 9 of 10 prompts in both the two- and three-constraint sets passing, for an overall success rate of 94%. Notably, the drops in formulation and convergence did not translate into comparable drops in verification, reinforcing that PLUTO can produce trajectories that satisfy the prompt even when intermediate stages of the pipeline are imperfect. Breaking down solver termination by constraint type reveals where convergence issues arise, as shown in Fig. 3. Each bar represents the eight prompts in which that constraint type appears, so a multi-constraint prompt is counted once 6Figure 3 : Solver termination by constraint type. Red outlines mark the three runs that failed Prompt Verification (F1–F3). under each of its types. As a result, the figure shows 80 constraint occurrences rather than the 50 individual prompts. Convex types such as keep-in regions (T02) and speed lim-its (T06) converged optimally in every prompt, while non-optimal terminations were concentrated in prompts contain-ing T10 constraints, which account for six of the eight non-optimal runs. The non-convex keep-out zone (T01) con-verged optimally in six of its eight prompts, and its two non-optimal runs still passed verification, indicating that the auto-convexification step handles standard non-convex constraints effectively. Most notably, every run that converged optimally also passed verification, while three of the eight non-optimal runs failed. Optimal Convergence is therefore a strong in-dicator of success, whereas non-optimal convergence signals that a trajectory warrants closer review rather than indicating failure outright. Of the five prompts whose constraint formulations differed from the reference, two still passed Prompt Verification. In the first, a “corridor” expected to be modeled as convex planar bounds was instead written as a keep-in cylinder. Because this difference stems from the interpretation of the word “corridor,” the agent’s output is not incorrect, and the resulting trajectory satisfied the prompt. In the second, the agent’s interpretation of the word “gradual” changed the implementation but not the verification outcome. Since the agent has no access to the reference or the constraint table, some variation in interpretation is expected, and these cases show that such variation does not always lead to failure. The remaining three deviations, however, did result in verification failures. Although many trajectories passed verification despite either a formulation mismatch or non-optimal convergence, all three Prompt Verification failures exhibited both, suggesting that failure arose from compounding issues rather than a single deviation. Another angle to consider is that as shown in Fig. 3, every failure also involved a T10 constraint, indicat-ing that complex or uncommon constraints remain the most challenging for the agent to formulate correctly. The failures were also spread evenly across complexity levels, with one each among the single-, two-, and three-constraint prompts, indicating that they were driven by the constraint type rather than the number of constraints. Two of the three failures involved the same T10 constraint, an out-of-plane exclusion, and resulted from the same formula-tion error. The prompt stated that the chaser should remain on 

either side of an exclusion plane. The intended interpretation was to select one side and impose a single convex half-space constraint, a simplification that satisfies the requirement while keeping the problem convex. In both cases, the agent instead attempted to encode the full disjunction, producing a non-convex formulation that led to non-optimal convergence and failed verification. In one of these runs, the agent identified the simpler one-sided formulation but set it aside in favor of a more general solution that preserved both options. The remaining failure followed a similar pattern. The agent began with a correct constraint, but when it encountered a blocker, it added complexity until the formulation became incorrect. Selecting a better free parameter after the initial failure would likely have succeeded. These failures highlight a core challenge in applying agents to fields that rely heavily on domain expertise. A central part of aerospace engineering practice is recognizing when a simpler formulation is sufficient to meet a requirement, rather than over-engineering a solution to the problem. In these cases, the agent favored completeness over sufficiency, overlook-ing valid simplifications that an experienced engineer would likely have made. Much of this judgment comes from experience, which the agent did not have during testing. All prompts were run on a clean version of PLUTO’s directory with no stored context from previous runs, ensuring that each prompt was evaluated on equal footing but also leaving the agent without accumulated guidance on simplifications like those described above. A benefit of PLUTO’s context-rich structure is that this kind of “tools of the trade” knowledge can be encoded into its reference materials, making such shortcuts more accessible to the agent. As tips and lessons from previous runs accumulate with continued use, failures of this kind are expected to become less frequent. Over time, a growing library of formulation strategies could also lead to new and more efficient ways to represent constraints and desired ren-dezvous behaviors. Some aspects of the results are harder to capture in numbers, particularly PLUTO’s visual outputs. Fig. 4 shows the output plots for a two-constraint prompt combining a waypoint constraint with a per-maneuver delta-v limit. The trajectory in Fig. 4a illustrates the nontrivial path required to reach the designated waypoint while keeping each individual maneuver within the delta-v limit, and Fig. 4b confirms that the limit is satisfied. These plots keep the user in the loop throughout the design process by showing directly how the intent of each prompt was interpreted and represented in the trajectory. Making these outputs a core part of the tool ensures that, while the agent accelerates constraint formulation, the user retains control over the design and can verify that each trajectory reflects their intent.

This paper introduces PLUTO, an agentic framework that integrates an AI coding tool with an SCP backend to enable language-driven trajectory design. Rather than asking the 7(a) Trajectory plot for the chaser and target. (b) Verification plot for the maneuver limit constraint.

The proposed framework therefore reduces the formulation burden on the user without relin-quishing the guarantees provided by numerical optimization and explicit verification. On the Prompt Verification metric, a 94% success rate indicates that this separation enables reli-able translation of high-level user intent into feasible trajec-tory design formulations while remaining robust to imperfec-tions in intermediate agent outputs.

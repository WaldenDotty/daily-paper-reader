## Abstract 

Reinforcement learning offers the prospect of a reusable sequential decision-making mechanism for spacecraft trajectory design, motivating policy interfaces that connect learned decisions to the underlying maneuver geometry. This paper develops Reachability Analysis-Informed Reinforcement Learning (RARL) for deterministic multi-impulse interplanetary transfers, placing intermediate waypoint selection at the center of the learned decision process. Local first-order reachability maps bounded velocity perturbations into an ellipsoidal set of next-node positions, within which the policy selects its waypoint. Lambert reconstruction then determines the corresponding maneuver to reach this selected waypoint along a dynamically consistent ballistic arc, coupling learned transfer-geometry selection with classical astrodynamics. A terminal two-impulse reconstruction completes the ren-dezvous, supported by a linear maneuver-demand assessment used for reward shaping. Numerical studies characterize this interface on a two-body Earth–Mars benchmark. Across three independent training runs, RARL achieves a mean maneuver cost of 10.23 km/s, 1.72% above a validated local sequential convex programming reference. Training over dispersed initial states extends policy reuse across a departure family with fixed target state and transfer duration. Each of the three indepen-dently trained multi-state policies completes all 10,000 held-out Monte Carlo departures without impulse-cap violations, compared with a mean feasibility rate of 6.49% for single-state policies. This broader sampled feasibility is accompanied by a 0.61% increase in mean nominal maneuver cost, without further training across departures. These results demonstrate that a reachability-informed de-cision interface supports benchmark-quality trajectory construction and policy reuse across dispersed departure conditions. 

Keywords Interplanetary transfer, Lambert problem, reachability analysis, reinforcement learning, trajectory optimization, spacecraft guidance, multi-impulse transfers. 

## 1 Introduction 

Even in deterministic settings, interplanetary transfers are shaped by nonlinear dynamics, limited maneuver authority, and stringent terminal requirements. In a multi-impulse rendezvous, these requirements couple the allocation of individual maneuvers to the geometry and cost of the complete transfer. Classical indirect optimal-control methods and direct transcription provide established tools for computing such trajectories [1, 2]. Their development also extends to joint trajectory and feedback design under uncertainty [3]. Alongside the design of an individual reference trajectory, mission analysis often requires the assessment of a family of departure conditions. This motivates complementing trajectory optimization with reusable decision mappings that can generate transfers from different initial states without solving the complete optimization problem anew for each departure. Reinforcement learning (RL) offers a means of constructing these mappings by learning a state-to-action policy through simulated interaction [4]. The main attraction is the opportunity to perform policy training offline and reuse the resulting

mapping across the conditions for which it has been trained and assessed. Within the broader application of artificial intelligence to spacecraft guidance, dynamics, and control [5], this approach has been explored for interplanetary trajectory design under uncertainty [6, 7] and autonomous cislunar orbit transfers [8]. These applications establish a basis for investigating how learned policies can accommodate variations in transfer conditions while meeting maneuver and terminal requirements. A complementary line of work embeds learning within an established guidance structure. Reinforced Q-laws [9] and Lyapunov controllers for lunar transfers [10] use learned parameters to adapt the underlying guidance law. Reinforced Lyapunov guidance has also been combined with precomputed ballistic capture trajectories for low-thrust cislunar transfers [11]. More recently, chance-constrained RL has been used to augment deterministic reference trajectories with learned feedforward and feedback corrections under sampled uncertainty [12]. These studies illustrate how learning can be assigned a defined role within a physically structured trajectory-design framework. For multi-impulse transfers, a related opportunity is to incorporate the relationship between maneuver authority and attainable transfer geometry directly into the policy’s decision space. This relationship is particularly relevant to rendezvous, where each maneuver changes both the subsequent ballistic arc and the maneuver demand required to meet the terminal state. Classical sensitivity maps and boundary-value solutions already describe aspects of this relationship [13, 14]. They therefore provide a basis for expressing a policy decision in terms of an intermediate target and reconstructing the maneuver required to reach it. The resulting design question is how to give the policy a geometrically meaningful set of next-node targets that reflects the available impulsive authority. Reachability analysis provides a natural basis for this construction by characterizing attainable states under specified dynamics and control bounds. Hamilton–Jacobi formulations describe reachable sets for nonlinear systems and dynamic games [15, 16], while ellipsoidal and zonotopic methods provide geometric representations and approximations of such sets [17, 18]. In astrodynamics, reachable domains have been studied for single-impulse maneuvers [19], tangent impulses subject to trajectory-safety restrictions [20], and ellipsoidal velocity-increment distributions [21]. This set-based view makes the connection between bounded maneuver authority and attainable spacecraft states explicit, providing a geometric description of where the spacecraft can go next. The use of such information in control decisions also connects to a broader literature on constraint satisfaction. Viability theory examines the ability to maintain state constraints over time [22], and verified feedback planning uses regions of attraction to organize admissible motion [23]. In RL, safety has been addressed through both learning objectives and exploration strategies [24]. Reachability-based approaches include iterative reachability estimation for policy optimization [25] and action projection using polynomial zonotopes [26]. The relationship between Hamilton–Jacobi reachability and RL is reviewed by Ganai et al. [27]. Together, these perspectives motivate making control constraints part of the representation used for learned decisions. Building on this viewpoint, this paper develops Reachability Analysis-Informed Reinforcement Learning (RARL) for deterministic multi-impulse interplanetary transfers. The central idea is to organize the learned decisions around intermediate waypoint selection, using local reachability geometry to define the available target set and deterministic astrodynamics to reconstruct the corresponding maneuvers. This places the selection of transfer geometry at the center of the policy’s role while retaining dynamical reconstruction within an explicit physical model. The reachability description remains local, and the feasibility of the resulting nonlinear transfers is assessed empirically. The contributions of this paper are fourfold. First, a reachability-informed action interface is formulated that relates bounded impulsive authority to node-dependent local waypoint sets. Second, a deterministic reconstruction layer connects policy-selected waypoints to nonlinear impulsive arcs, separating geometric decision making from maneuver reconstruction. Third, a terminal two-impulse proxy is formulated to assess the remaining rendezvous maneuver demand relative to the available control authority. Fourth, analytical and numerical studies examine the action mapping, the adequacy of the local reachability approximation, and the interpretation of the terminal proxy. These studies provide evidence for the principal modeling and interface choices in the proposed formulation. The framework is evaluated on a deterministic Earth–Mars benchmark using the problem data of Marmo et al. [28], with a separately computed sequential convex programming (SCP) solution serving as the numerical reference. Evaluation extends from nominal single-state training to a family of initial states induced by launch-time offsets, with the target state and transfer duration held fixed. Three independent training runs are performed for each regime, and the selected policies are assessed on a common held-out set of 10 4 Monte Carlo (MC) departures. Reporting both individual policies and aggregate statistics distinguishes variation across independent training runs from variation across departure states within each policy. This establishes the consistency of the observed performance across the trained policies and quantifies the empirical robustness–cost tradeoff associated with extending policy reuse beyond the nominal departure condition. 2The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 defines the deterministic transfer problem, and Section 3 develops the RARL formulation. Section 4 examines the action interface and its supporting approximations. Section 5 presents the Earth–Mars configuration and the single-state and multi-state results. Section 6 concludes the paper.

## 3 Reachability Analysis-Informed Reinforcement Learning Methodology 

The proposed RARL framework combines learned waypoint selection with deterministic reconstruction of each transfer segment. At every policy-controlled node, a local state-transition map defines coordinates for the next position under bounded impulsive authority. The policy selects a waypoint in this coordinate system, after which a Lambert solve reconstructs the maneuver and ballistic arc required to reach it. A deterministic terminal solve then completes the position and velocity rendezvous.

During evaluation, actions are selected deterministically from the mean of the policy distribution and passed through the action mapping in Eq. (16). The state and STM are integrated together using the DOP853 solver in SciPy [42], with relative and absolute tolerances of 10 −12 and 10 −13 , respectively. Each convex subproblem minimizes the sum of impulse magnitudes subject to the affine dynamics, the prescribed initial and terminal conditions, and the impulse bounds. Euclidean trust regions constrain changes from the reference state and impulse at each node, with nondimensional radii of 0.1 and 0.02 , respectively. A numerical margin of 10 −9 km /s is subtracted from the physical impulse cap within the optimizer. The resulting second-order cone programs are formulated in CVXPY [43, 44] and solved using MOSEK [45], with primal feasibility, dual feasibility and relative-gap tolerances of 10 −10 .The initialization originates from a two-impulse Lambert transfer between the prescribed endpoint positions, with zero intermediate impulses. A stored SCP solution obtained from this initialization is subsequently refined to the tolerances specified here. After each convex solve, the updated impulses are propagated from the prescribed initial state under the nonlinear dynamics to construct the next reference. Convergence requires terminal position and velocity errors no greater than 10 −3 km and 10 −6 km /s, respectively, with no physical impulse-cap exceedance. It also requires a maximum absolute componentwise difference of 10 −11 between the optimized and nonlinearly propagated nondimensional node states, and a change in total maneuver cost no greater than 10 −8 km /s.13 The nominal refinement converges in four SCP iterations. Independent propagation of the resulting impulse sequence using PyKEP [36] gives terminal position and velocity errors of 2.19 × 10 −4 km and 2.20 × 10 −11 km /s, respectively, with all impulses within the physical cap. This solution provides the validated local SCP reference used in the subsequent performance comparison.

## 6 Conclusion 

This paper presented Reachability Analysis-Informed Reinforcement Learning (RARL) for deterministic multi-impulse interplanetary transfers. The formulation places intermediate waypoint selection at the center of the learned decision process, using a local first-order reachability map to define the waypoint set and Lambert solutions to reconstruct the corresponding nonlinear transfer segments. This gives the policy a geometrically interpretable decision space while retaining classical astrodynamics for maneuver reconstruction. The accompanying diagnostics examine the action geometry, local approximation error, and terminal maneuver-demand assessment, making the role and limitations of this interface explicit. In the Earth–Mars case study, this formulation recovers benchmark-quality nominal performance across three indepen-dent training runs, with a mean maneuver cost 1.72% above the SCP reference. Training over dispersed initial states extends this capability beyond the nominal departure condition. Each of the three independently trained multi-state policies completes all 10 4 Monte Carlo departures without truncation or impulse-cap violations, using the same selected policy across departures without further training. This broader sampled feasibility is accompanied by a 0.61% increase in mean nominal maneuver cost relative to single-state training, while retaining the approximate bang–off–bang allocation. The resulting empirical robustness–cost tradeoff shows how the same reachability-informed formulation can support both nominal specialization and policy reuse across a family of departure conditions. These findings concern a two-body Earth–Mars transfer with launch-time-induced initial-state dispersion, a fixed target state, and a fixed transfer duration. Within this setting, nonlinear feasibility is established empirically, and the 20 reachability map remains a local approximation. Extending the formulation to broader transfer families, adaptive node placement, and refined reachability approximations would help assess its applicability to longer or more nonlinear arcs. The reusable state-feedback mapping also offers potential use as an onboard high-level guidance layer coupled to deterministic reconstruction, or as a structured initializer for subsequent trajectory optimization. Both applications build on the central contribution demonstrated here, a policy interface that couples learned selection of the transfer geometry with deterministic reconstruction of the maneuvers required to realize it.

# TEMPUS: Relativistic coordinate time scales for any solar-system body from arbitrary ephemerides 

Y. Sey ff ert and A. Fienga 

Observatoire de la Côte d’Azur, Université Côte d’Azur, CNRS, Géoazur, IRD, 250 avenue A. Einstein 06560 Valbonne, France Received XX; accepted XX 

ABSTRACT 

Context. Precise time-scale transformations between solar-system bodies are an essential requirement for deep-space science and solar system navigation. The post-Newtonian relativistic expression relating a body-centered coordinate time TCX to the Barycentric Coordinate Time TCB depends on the gravitational environment and velocity of the target body, and must be evaluated consistently with the chosen planetary ephemerides. So far only INPOP has provided such a solution as part of its ephemerides releases, albeit only for Earth with TCG-TCB. 

Aims. We present T empus , a tool that numerically integrates the IAU 2000 time-transformation for any solar-system body using positions and velocities from an arbitrary ephemerides. We provide a validated, ephemerides-agnostic implementation of TCX −TCB and assess its accuracy against published independent solutions. 

Methods. The rate d TCX /d TCB is integrated with an Adams–Moulton order-12 predictor–corrector. The gravitational potential sum tested here includes the Sun, eight planets, Pluto, up to 343 main-belt asteroids, and 30 trans-Neptunian objects. Outputs can be stored in readable format tables of sampled values or as Chebyshev coe ffi cient files. Comparisons are performed against the TU Dresden time solutions (Klioner 2025), the TCG-TCB solution provided with INPOP19a (Fienga et al. 2019) and INPOP21a (Fienga et al. 2021a), and the LTE440 lunar solution (Lu et al. 2025). We use INPOP19a, INPOP21a, DE430, DE440, and EPM2021 as input ephemerides. 

Results. For the planetary bodies T empus agrees with Klioner (2025) at the 10 −21 s s −1 level over 200 yr. Against the INPOP19a released TCG-TCB we also agree to the same level. The main-belt asteroids as well as the trans-Neptunian objects shift the planetary time scales at the 10 −18 s s −1 level, and the choice of planetary and lunar ephemerides contributes di ff erences on the 10 −18 s s −1 level as well. 

Conclusions. Tempus provides a validated, ephemeris-agnostic post-Newtonian time-transformation for any solar-system body.

1. Introduction 

According to general relativity, a clock’s tick rate depends on its velocity and the gravitational potential at its location: a clock co-moving with the Earth therefore runs at a slightly di ff erent rate than an ideal clock co-moving with the Moon, Mars, or solar-system barycenter (SSB). For the Earth this secular drift to the SSB is about 1 .28 ms per day, and an additional periodic compo-nent (driven by orbital eccentricity and planetary perturbations) that must be modeled to nanosecond accuracy for modern space applications. With the IAU 2000 resolution So ff el et al. (2003), the IAU gives the metric that should be used for describing the motions of objects in the solar system weak field approximation. Derived from the So ff el et al. (2003) metric and considering each specific gravitational environment, one can define di ff erent time-scales: Barycentric Coordinate Time (TCB) at the SSB, its body-mass-centered analogues Geocentric Coordinate Time (TCG), Seleno-centric Coordinate Time (TCL), and generically TCX for any body X at its center of mass, with TCX −TCB typically fixed to zero at the 1977 origin, when the SI second came into e ff ect. Closely related to TCB is Barycentric Dynamical Time (TDB), which is TCB rescaled to best match the secular drift of clocks at Earth’s geoid. Deep-space radio science and navigation need these time scales in practice: the proper time kept by a clock at a body X must be tied to the barycentric frame through the same ephemerides and perturber set that propagate the spacecraft and model the signal path, since any mismatch leaks into the esti-mated range. As range is inferred from a light travel time, 1 ns corresponds to about 30 cm, so centimetre-level ranging requires TCX −TCB at the sub-nanosecond level, where the second post-Newtonian terms become relevant – besides being there for bet-ter consistency. Rotation modeling is attached to the body-fixed frame and in consequence, in GR, this body-fixed frame has a specific 4-D metric, di ff erent from the metric attached to the Barycentric Celestial Reference System (BCRS). Consequently, the time-scale attached to the body-fixed frame in which the Ro-tation modeling should be described, is di ff erent from the TCB or TDB time-scales. However, so far, no rotation modeling ex-cept the one for the Earth for which the rotation angles are given relative to TT or TCG, is produced in the natural local time TCX but in TDB. So far, no general tool exists for computing time-scale for any body that a user can apply to an arbitrary body, with an ar-bitrary ephemerides, and with a controllable set of perturbers. We present Tempus , an open-source tool that fills this gap. It numerically integrates the IAU 2000 time-transformation from an arbitrary planetary ephemerides, and is (i) ephemeris-agnostic , reading INPOP, DE, and EPM files with either TDB or TCB as initial time-scale; (ii) body-agnostic , computing TCX −TCB for any target X, or the di ff erence between two body-centered scales such as TCG −TCL directly; (iii) perturber-

The remainder of the paper describes the model and its implementation (Sect. 2), with some parameterization caveats discussed in Sect. 3, then validates T empus against indepen-dent solutions (Sect. 4), studies its sensitivity to the input ephemerides and perturbers (Sect. 5), describes the Chebyshev polynomial output representation option (Sect. 6), and then dis-cusses the timing implications for planetary rotation and naviga-tion (Sect. 7) before concluding (Sect. 8).

2.3. ephemerides compatibility 

All ephemerides access goes through CALCEPH (v4.0.5; Gastineau et al. 2015), so T empus reads INPOP binary files (as .dat or .tcheb ) and SPICE kernels ( .bsp with the as-sociated .tpc /.tls ) through a single interface. The timescale of the file is detected from its header. TCB ephemerides are used directly; for a TDB ephemerides T empus applies the ex-act linear rescaling of positions, velocities, and GM values by

1/(1 − LB), with LB = 1.550519768 × 10 −8 (Petit & Luzum 2010), so that the transformation is always evaluated in TCB-consistent units. Epochs are converted between TT, TDB, and TCB by reading each file’s own internal time-ephemerides series rather than an external model, keeping the result self-consistent with the ephemerides in use. All epochs are carried as split (in-teger + fractional) Julian dates (JD) to preserve sub-nanosecond resolution, which an ordinary float64 JD cannot represent. The ephemerides tested here are INPOP19a (TCB and TDB) Fienga et al. (2019), INPOP21a (TCB and TDB) Fienga et al. (2021a), DE430 Folkner et al. (2014), DE440 Park et al. (2021), and EPM2021 Pitjeva et al. (2022). 

> 2.4. Numerical integration

The transformation rate of Eq. (1) is integrated with an Adams–Moulton predictor–corrector of order 12 (AM12) by de-fault. Because the integrand is a smooth function of interpo-lated ephemerides states, a high-order multistep method con-verges far better, while the total runtime does not increase significantly — as the quadrature here is a small fraction of the total runtime dominated by ephemerides evaluation calls. AM12 is the default after comparison with the trapezoidal rule and lower-order schemes ( rk4 , am4 –am10 , all selectable through --integrator ). The 11-step start-up phase of AM12 is prepended before the requested start epoch, such that the re-ported output series is free of start-up transients. All results here use an integration step size of 0 .75 d (18 h), matching the internal sampling rate of the ephemerides like INPOP. 

> 2.5. Chebyshev polynomial output

For downstream use the integrated samples can be fitted, by the tempus chebyshev subcommand, into a piecewise Cheby-shev series and written as an ASCII coe ffi cient file ( .asc ) in the same tabular layout as the series distributed by INPOP and Klioner (2025), which T empus also reads directly for compar-ison. The subcommand can also write the series in either of the two time-argument parameterisations distributed with the TU Dresden time solutions (see Sect. 3). The fitting algorithm, its tuning options, and the representation and evaluator accuracy are described in Sect. 6. To keep the fitting and physical error budgets separate, all comparisons in Sect. 4 use the direct T em -

pus integration results directly, without Chebyshev fitting. 

3. Coordinate and parameterisation choices 

Two options in T empus change not the accuracy but the very quantity that is computed, and both are easy to misuse. We high-light them here because a user who overlooks this would incur errors and inconsistencies far larger than the sub-nanosecond model accuracy established in Sect. 4. 

> 3.1. Differences between two body-centered scales

By default the integration is centered on the solar-system barycenter ( --center SSB ), giving TCX −TCB at the body X center of mass. Setting --center to a body and --target to another — for example --center EARTH_CENTER --target MOON — makes T empus run two barycentric integrations and sub-tract them, returning the di ff erence between two time-scales at the centers of mass of the two bodies, here TCG −TCL, as a func-tion of TDB or TCB (depending on what timescale the under-lying ephemerides is parameterised with, typically TDB). This subtraction is exact as a function of TDB or TCB .One must be aware though, that this di ff erence is only valid in the simultaneity of the BCRS. To move to a di ff erent notion of simultaneity, the user must add a kinematic boundary term. In the case of TCL, it would be vE · rLE /c2, that moves to a geocentric-simultaneity description. This term has an amplitude of ∼ 125 μs oscillating at the lunar month, and is only a func-tion of the ephemerides built from the same state vectors as the Tempus integration itself. Tempus deliberately leaves this term out of the default output and provides the function tempus.model.boost_term to add it when a particular simultaneity convention is required. The two descriptions are di ff erent functions of time; consequently a naive subtraction of two TCX −TCB series (staying in the simultaneity of the BCRS), compared against a reference such as the lunar solution in Kopeikin & Kaplan (2024) (which carries a GCRS notion of simultaneity), will disagree by this ∼ 125 μs term. This is expected behaviour, not an error. What has been described so far has exclusively been center-of-mass quantities. A real clock, at the surface or in orbit, needs one further term c−2(−v2clock /2 − WX (xclock )), which unlike the boundary term above is not a function of the ephemerides: it requires a gravity-field model of body X and a clock loca-tion /trajectory, and thus lies outside the scope of T empus and is a users responsibility. This term dominates any clock comparison, and is applied on top of (each) TCX −TCB. 

> 3.2. Choice of time argument

The o ff set TCX −TCB tabulated as a function of TCB /TDB and the same o ff set tabulated as a function of the body’s own time at its center of mass TCX are two di ff erent functions. They di ff er by a secular term F · ∆ ≈ L2 

> X

τ that grows linearly with elapsed time τ and quadratically with the body’s rate constant LX : about 0.69 μs cy −1 for TCG and 0 .30 μs cy −1 for TCM (Klioner 2026). Evaluating a series built against one argument at a numerically equal value of the other therefore introduces an error of exactly this magnitude — microseconds per century, orders of magni-tude above the model accuracy. Any user of a time-ephemerides product must know which argument parameterises it. Tempus makes both available. The integration and its na-tive ASCII export are parameterised by the ephemerides time argument (TCB for a TCB ephemerides, TDB for a TDB one); 

tempus chebyshev --also-tcx additionally writes the o ff set parameterised by the body’s own coordinate time TCX. Because every integrated sample is a single event carrying both time la-bels, this re-parameterisation is an exact per-event relabelling of the output ( tempus.model.relabel_to_tcx ) rather than a re-integration, with --anchor aligning the relabelled axis to the 1977 origin. This re-labeling applied either to a T empus run, or separately to the TCB-parameterised ASCII file distributed by Klioner (2025), reproduces Klioner’s independently-integrated, TCX-parameterised (“inverse”) product at the picosecond level. 

4. Validation 

We validate T empus by comparing its output against other solu-tions: the independent TU Dresden solution (Klioner 2025), the TCG −TCB series stored inside the ephemerides files themselves INPOP19a and INPOP21a, and the LTE440 lunar time solution (Lu et al. 2025). As will be seen, these comparisons show di ff er-ences far below the accuracy of the modern atomic clocks, and validate our implementation with T empus .

4.3. Comparison with LTE440. 

The lunar time solution LTE440 (Lu et al. 2025) is an in-dependent product built on DE440, with the claim it com-pletely reproduces DE440’s dynamical model. Run on that same DE440 ephemerides, T empus reproduces it to 1 .64 × 10 −17 s s −1

(52 ns cy −1) with a detrended peak of 0 .043 ns. This di ff erence is not a floor: it falls from 2 .38 to 1 .64 × 10 −17 s s −1 as the 343 main-belt asteroids and the 30 individual TNOs are added ver-sus a planets-only run, with the detrended peak dropping by a factor 4 .5. It therefore still measures the remaining di ff erence in dynamical model rather than the transformation itself. 5. Sensitivity study 

The transformation rate of Eq. (1) is built entirely from the masses, positions, and velocities of the bodies that make up the solar system: the potential UX (Eq. 3) and the c−4 cross-terms (Eq. 4) sum over every perturber with a provided GM and trajec-tory. The computed time scale is therefore only as good as its un-derlying dynamical model, and crucially that model must be kept 

consistent with the ephemerides being used: T empus should eval-uate the potential from the same bodies, with the same masses and trajectories, that were used to construct the ephemerides in the first place. A mismatch between the two body sets does not degrade the numerical accuracy of the transformation; it injects a physical signal — the gravitational e ff ect of the bodies present in one model but absent in the other. Two levers control this dynamical model, and we vary them independently. The choice of planetary ephemerides fixes the masses and orbits of the Sun and planets in a mutually consis-tent way (Sect. 5.1). On top of a given ephemerides, the optional small-body perturbers — main-belt asteroids (MBAs) and trans-Neptunian /Kuiper-belt objects (TNOs or KBOs) — and the solar oblateness can be switched on and o ff (Sects. 5.2 and 5.3), iso-lating each contribution while the planetary motion is held fixed. We quantify the sensitivity of TCX −TCB to each. One limitation applies throughout. Modern ephemerides may model parts of the Kuiper belt as an ideal ring of evenly spaced and equal mass point masses on fixed Keplerian orbits, rather than an extended set of actual small bodies. Such a treat-ment is not yet supported by T empus .

> 5.3. Effect of the solar oblateness

The Sun’s quadrupole moment J2,⊙ enters the c−2 potential (Sect. 2.1). Its e ff ect on the planetary time scales is negligi-ble: for the Earth (TCG) on INPOP19a it contributes a drift of 2.1 × 10 −20 s s −1, with a residual within ±0.6 ps over 200 years. This is four orders of magnitude below the β term of Eq. (1) and two under e ff ect of small bodies. It is o ff by default, however a user may enable it with --sun-j2 .

6. Chebyshev representation 

The integrated samples are the primary output of T empus , but the form in which TCX −TCB is distributed and consumed down-stream is a piecewise Chebyshev polynomial series in a plain-text ASCII coe ffi cient file (Sect. 2.5). This section describes the fitting algorithm, the options that control it, and the accuracy of the resulting Chebyshev product, in both the representation error of approximating the integrated series by these polynomials, and the evaluation error of reading those coe ffi cients back. 

> 6.1. Fitting algorithm

The span is divided into multiple fixed-length segments, called 

granules (default 4 d), each with a fitted Chebyshev series of fixed degree. Within a granule the fit is a collocation rather than a least-squares problem: n+1 collocation nodes are placed on [ −1, 1], the T empus samples are interpolated to those nodes, and the Chebyshev coe ffi cients follow from a single small linear solve 

c = M−1f, where M is the Vandermonde matrix of the basis at the nodes. Because M−1 depends only on the degree and node distri-bution, it is computed once and cached, so fitting each granule costs one matrix–vector product; the granules are independent and fitted in parallel. Evaluation uses the Clenshaw recurrence, and all times are carried relative to the granule start to avoid can-cellation against the large ( ∼ 2.4 × 10 6) Julian-date base. The mentioned interpolation of the T empus integration sam-ples to the nodes is done with a local stencil of --npoints 

neighbouring samples (default 10). If the integration rate 

d TCX /d TCB is exported alongside the values ( integrate --out-rate ), a Hermite interpolant using both value and derivative may be used; otherwise a Lagrange interpolant on the values alone is used. Three node distributions are available (--dist ): Gauss–Lobatto with endpoints (default), the interior Chebyshev–Gauss roots, and a C1-continuous Lobatto variant that additionally matches the derivative at the granule boundaries — enforcing continuity of both value and rate across granules — which requires the rate column. 

> 6.2. Tuning the fit

The representation error is governed by the polynomial degree (--order ) and the granule length ( --granule-len ): raising the degree or shortening the granule both reduce the residual, trad-ing file size for accuracy. T empus gates the fit on two indepen-dent quality checks. The first is a truncation estimate from the decay of the trailing Chebyshev coe ffi cients, which flags a gran-ule whose series has not converged. The second is an explicit residual budget ( --max-err ): the maximum |fit − samples | over the whole span must stay below a user-set threshold. The budget is the more reliable gate near machine precision, where the coef-ficient tail flattens into the float64 noise floor and the truncation test can report non-convergence for an otherwise excellent fit. When a gate fails, --auto retries with progressively higher de-gree and halved granule length until the budget is met. Finally, 

--anchor zeros the series at a chosen epoch (bare --anchor 

uses the 1977 origin) by shifting only the constant coe ffi cient of each granule, as long as the T empus integration run covers that chosen anchor epoch. 

> 6.3. Representation and evaluator accuracy

The representation error is not a fixed property of the method but a setting: it is bounded by the residual budget --max-err , which the fit is gated on (Sect. 6.2), and can be pushed down by raising the degree or shortening the granule at the cost of file size. A user can therefore choose it to sit as far below the physical error budget of Sect. 4 as the application requires. The evaluator is validated independently against CALCEPH. An INPOP time-ephemerides ASCII is an export of the Cheby-shev coe ffi cients stored inside the binary ephemerides, so read-ing it with the T empus evaluator and querying the same quan-tity through CALCEPH exercises two implementations – our Clenshaw recurrence implemented as part of T empus and CAL-CEPH’s own recurrence – on identical coe ffi cients. Over 10 5 ran-dom epochs spanning all granules of the INPOP19a TCG −TCB file the two agree to at most one unit in the last place ( ∼ 4 ×

10 −16 s), with 99 .99% of epochs bit-identical, confirming that the evaluation adds no error above float64 rounding: reading a dis-tributed series back costs nothing against the model accuracy. 

7. Applications 

A body’s coordinate time has two kinds of use, and we give one example of each. The first is a modeling question independent of any mission: rotation models are currently referred to TDB while the rotation itself belongs in the body’s own time scale, and Sect. 7.1 quantifies the di ff erence. The second is operational: the radio navigation systems being designed for the Moon, Mars need a body time that is traceable to Earth at the nanosecond level, and Sect. 7.2 asks whether the transformation meets that. Tempus supplies the transformation only; it does not replace the orbit determination, tracking or rotation models that consume it. 

> 7.1. Relativistic timescales for planetary rotation

A body’s rotation must be modeled in the frame attached to it, known as the body-fixed frame. In general relativity, this frame is defined by an origin at the body’s center of mass, a local metric determined by the body’s own gravitational field, and an orientation that evolves under the external gravitational torque from other bodies. 7.2. Time scales for lunar and Martian navigation 

The application that motivated T empus is the Position, Naviga-tion and Timing (PNT) service, and in particular, the Lunanet constellation at the Moon (Lunanet Interoperability Specifica-tion for PNT working group 2025) and its ESA Martian coun-terpart MARCONI (Melman et al. 2024), in whose preparatory study T empus was developed. A user derives one-way ranges from the constellation’s clocks, which are kept on a system time traceable to Earth, so an error in the transformation between the body’s coordinate time and TT enters the user’s timing directly, at about 30 cm of range per 1 ns. For the Moon the IAU has defined TCL (IAU XXXII 2024); LunaNet requires every provider to deliver timing synchronised to a standard lunar time, or the corrections needed to reach it (Lunanet Interoperability Specification for PNT working group 2025); and the architectures studied for ESA Lunanet contribu-tion (Moonlight) tie the satellite clocks to the constellation ref-erence at the 1–5 ns level, within a 10 m signal-in-space budget (Iess et al. 2025; Sesta et al. 2025). MARCONI aims to o ff er the same kind of one-way ranging at Mars, with expected accuracies of 15 m for a surface user aided by a digital elevation model, be-low 100 m for a lander and 80 m or better for an orbiter (Melman et al. 2024). The transformation these systems have to undergo is not small. TCL runs slower than TCG by 1 .48 μs per day (from Table 1), with periodic terms of up to 0 .47 μs from the eccentric-ity of the lunar orbit (Turyshev et al. 2025), so a nanosecond is 0.2% of that amplitude; TCM runs faster than TCG by 0 .44 ms per day. Four sources contribute to the error of a computed series, each bounded by one of the comparisons of Sects. 4 and 5, and they span four orders of magnitude. Largest is the truncation of the model itself: the β term of Eq. (1) contributes 1 .1×10 −16 s s −1

(block (a) of Table 3), or 3 .5 ns per year, so a transformation carried only to c−2 with the α contribution only leaves the re-quirement within the year. Next is the completeness of the dy-namical model: with all 373 small bodies of DE440 supplied, Tempus still di ff ers from LTE440 by 1 .6 × 10 −17 s s −1, or 0 .5 ns per year (Table 2), which is the part of DE440’s lunar model that Tempus does not carry. Smaller again is the impact of choosing di ff erent ephemerides, 1 .3–1 .9 × 10 −18 s s −1 for the Moon and up to 2 .4 × 10 −18 for Mars, below 0 .1 ns per year, though with de-trended peaks reaching 1 .7 ns over 200 yr (block (c); Sect. 5.1). The smallest possible error is from T empus numerical inte-gration itself, shown by the comparison with the TU Dresden solution: both use the same INPOP19a ephemerides on the same body set, so only their implementations di ff er by at most 6 .9 ps for TCL and 5 .0 ps for TCM (Table 1) for the peak di ff erence. Therefore, against 1–5 ns, only the lunar dynamical model, at half a nanosecond per year, is relevant for how long a computed series stays inside the requirement. The distinction between the drift and the max peaks of the residual, properties given in all Tables, matters here: a drift is in part absorbed by the periodic re-synchronisation such a system can perform, whereas the residual peaks are not. A constellation time scale should in any case be computed from the same ephemerides that produce its satellite orbits. Then there is no mismatch between the two, and the com-pleteness of the dynamical model is left as the term to improve. 

8. Conclusions 

With T empus we provide a validated, ephemeris-agnostic im-plementation of the IAU 2000 time transformation, Eq. (1), for any solar-system body at its center of mass. TCX −TCB is inte-grated numerically from any available ephemerides (INPOP, DE or EPM) in their TDB or TCB releases, with dynamical model-ing scalable by the user, and is tabulated against the time scale of that ephemerides or, as the TU Dresden time solutions do (Klioner 2025), against TCX itself (Sect. 3.2), written directly or as a Chebyshev polynomial product. Every run is reproducible from a single command line. The implementation of Eq. (1) has been validated against the TU Dresden time solutions (Klioner 2025) and the TCG −TCB solution distributed with INPOP19a: over a 200 yr span the drifts agree at the 10 −21 s s −1 level and the detrended residuals stay be-low 25 ps (Tables 1 and 2), far below the accuracy of any current atomic clock. The comparison with LTE440 is limited instead by the remaining di ff erence in the lunar dynamical model, not by the integration of Eq. (1) itself. The five largest main-belt asteroids account for some 60% of the 4 .3–4 .7 × 10 −18 s s −1 by which the full main belt shifts TCX −TCB for the Earth, Mars and the Moon; the trans-Neptunian objects add a further 2–3 × 10 −18 ; and the choice of ephemerides contributes up to 2 .4 × 10 −18 .A body time scale is therefore only as good as its consistency with the ephemerides and body set that produced the trajectories it serves, and T empus makes that consistency a matter of passing the correct mass and trajectory files. Tempus has already been used for the ILuRS2026 lunar prod-ucts and in ESA’s MARCONI study. Against the nanosecond synchronisation that lunar and Martian navigation constellations require, neither our implementation of the numerical integra-tion nor the choice of input ephemerides is the limiting term; the completeness of the dynamical model is. It also supplies the time argument in which planetary rotation must be modelled (see Sec. 7.1). The T empus software will be made available as open source. Natural future extensions would be the introduction of the per-turbations induced by a ring, support and tests of natural satel-lite ephemerides with the parent planet’s oblateness for bodies such as Ganymede and Titan, and a surface time output option referred to a chosen geoid value.
